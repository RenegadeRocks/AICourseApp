# Wednesday Deep-dive: RAG from Naive to Production-Grade

## Why this matters (operator framing)

RAG is the mechanism behind "ask questions about your documents," "chatbot trained on our knowledge base," and "AI that knows our product." It is the number one use case clients ask for and the number one thing built badly. By building it yourself from scratch — embedding, indexing, retrieval, augmentation, generation — you will understand exactly where it breaks and how to fix it, which is a skill you can sell.

## Core content

### 1. First principles: why retrieval solves what fine-tuning doesn't

A language model's knowledge is frozen at training cutoff. It does not know:
- Your internal documents
- Events after its cutoff date
- Customer-specific data
- Proprietary research

You have two options: (a) put the knowledge in the model weights via fine-tuning, or (b) inject it at inference time via retrieval. Fine-tuning is expensive (compute, time, data labeling), rarely updated, and doesn't give you attribution — you can't point to the source of a claim. RAG is cheap per-update, always current, and every answer can be attributed to a specific document passage.

Lewis et al. 2020 proved the concept: a retrieve-then-generate model outperformed parametric-only models on open-domain QA, with the key advantage that retrieved evidence was inspectable[^1].

### 2. The naive RAG pipeline (and where it fails)

**Step 1: Chunking**
Split documents into overlapping chunks of ~256–512 tokens. Overlap by ~50 tokens so sentences aren't cut at chunk boundaries. Simple fixed-size chunking is fast but semantically blind — a chunk might end in the middle of a paragraph.

**Step 2: Embedding**
Convert each chunk to a dense vector using a sentence-transformers model (e.g., `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, `BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5`). The vector encodes semantic meaning. Similar meaning → similar vector → small cosine distance.

**Step 3: Indexing**
Store vectors in a vector database (FAISS for local dev; Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant for production). FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) builds an index over the vectors for fast approximate nearest-neighbor lookup[^2].

**Step 4: Query**
Embed the user's question with the same model. Compute cosine similarity between the question vector and all chunk vectors. Return the top-k most similar chunks.

**Step 5: Augment and generate**
Stuff the top-k chunks into the prompt as context. Ask the model to answer based only on the context.

**Where it fails:**
- A chunk says "the revenue was $2.3M" — but the adjacent chunk explains which quarter that refers to. Naive chunking split them apart. The model retrieves the first chunk and answers without the qualifier.
- A technical manual uses abbreviations defined in the preface; chunks from the body don't include those definitions.
- A question about "the Paris office" retrieves chunks about "Paris" that are really about a museum exhibit, not the company's office.

Wang et al. 2024 surveyed the state of RAG practice and identified chunk isolation as the leading cause of retrieval failure in production systems[^3].

### 3. Contextual retrieval: Anthropic's September 2024 breakthrough

In September 2024, Anthropic published research showing that augmenting each chunk with a brief contextual summary — generated by a language model before indexing — reduces retrieval failure rate by ~49% versus naive chunking alone[^4].

The idea: before embedding a chunk, prepend a context sentence:

```
Context: This chunk is from the Q3 2024 earnings report for Acme Corp. 
The document discusses quarterly revenue, operating expenses, and guidance.
This specific passage covers the breakdown of EMEA regional revenue.

[original chunk text follows]
```

This context is generated once at index time using a cheap model. The cost is ~$1–2 per million tokens to index a large knowledge base. The benefit: every chunk carries enough context to be retrieved on its own, even when the question uses vocabulary or framing that differs from the chunk.

The full Anthropic contextual retrieval approach combines three techniques:
1. **Contextual chunk enrichment** (described above)
2. **BM25 hybrid search** — sparse lexical retrieval (keyword matching) run in parallel with dense embedding search
3. **Reranking** — a cross-encoder model rescoring the top-k results before passing to the LLM

Anthropic's benchmarks showed: contextual enrichment alone = 35% fewer failures; + BM25 hybrid = 49% fewer failures; + reranking = 67% fewer failures versus naive embedding-only retrieval[^4].

### 4. BM25 and hybrid search

BM25 is a classical information retrieval algorithm (best match 25) that scores documents by term frequency and inverse document frequency[^5]. It works on exact keyword matches. Dense embedding search works on semantic similarity. They are complementary:

- User asks "what is the TF-IDF score for document X?" — BM25 finds the exact phrase; embedding search might retrieve conceptually similar but lexically different passages.
- User asks "what are the biggest risks in our supply chain?" — embedding search excels at conceptual matching; BM25 would miss it unless "risks" and "supply chain" appeared together.

Hybrid search: run both, merge results with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) or a weighted score. This is now standard practice for production RAG systems and supported natively in Pinecone, Weaviate, and Elasticsearch[^6].

### 5. Reranking: the second retrieval pass

Retrieval retrieves a rough top-k (say top-20). Reranking re-scores those 20 candidates against the question using a cross-encoder: a model that takes the (query, document) pair as joint input and outputs a relevance score. Cross-encoders are slower than bi-encoders (they can't pre-compute embeddings) but dramatically more accurate.

Popular rerankers: Cohere Rerank API, BAAI/bge-reranker-base (open source), cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 (HuggingFace).

The tradeoff: reranking adds 100–500ms latency per query. For interactive chat, this matters. For batch processing, it doesn't. Jason Liu (jxnl.co) recommends always measuring reranking's lift on your specific dataset before paying the latency cost — gains are highly domain-dependent[^7].

### 6. Evals: the part everyone skips

You cannot improve what you don't measure. A RAG pipeline without evals is a guess. The minimal evaluation suite for RAG:

**Retrieval recall**: for a labeled set of question-answer pairs, what percentage of questions have the correct chunk in the top-k results? Below 70% means your retrieval is broken regardless of how good your generation is.

**Answer correctness**: given the correct context was retrieved, how often does the model produce the correct answer? This isolates generation quality.

**Faithfulness**: does the model's answer contradict the context? (Hallucination detection.) Automated with a second LLM as judge.

**Groundedness**: is every claim in the answer traceable to a specific passage? Important for regulated industries.

Tools: RAGAS (open source, Python, measures all four automatically)[^8]; LLM-as-judge patterns from Anthropic's cookbook.

### 7. GraphRAG: when structure matters

For knowledge bases with complex entity relationships (e.g., a knowledge graph of organizations, people, and events), Microsoft Research's GraphRAG (2024) showed that augmenting vector search with graph traversal significantly improves answers to multi-hop questions[^9].

Example: "Which of our suppliers have connections to vendors we've had compliance issues with?" — this requires reasoning across multiple nodes in a relationship graph, which flat vector search handles poorly.

> My take: GraphRAG is impressive but operationally heavy. For 95% of business use cases, contextual retrieval + hybrid search is the right stopping point. Reach for GraphRAG when you have a genuine knowledge graph structure and multi-hop queries as a first-class requirement.

## Common mistakes experts see

- **Not measuring retrieval quality separately from generation quality**: if answers are wrong, 80% of the time it's retrieval, not generation — but most people only look at the final answer.
- **Chunking by file, not by semantic unit**: using entire pages as chunks, or splitting arbitrarily at 512 tokens without sentence-boundary awareness. Use sentence-aware splitting (e.g., NLTK's `sent_tokenize` or LangChain's `RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter`).
- **One embedding model for everything**: `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` is fast and general-purpose but underperforms domain-specific models for technical, legal, or medical text. Always benchmark on your actual domain.
- **Ignoring BM25**: the first 2-3 hours of implementing hybrid search will recover more failed queries than the next 2 weeks of prompt tuning.
- **Not handling the "no relevant context" case**: if retrieval returns low-confidence chunks, the model should say "I don't have information about this" rather than hallucinating from the low-quality context. Gate on a similarity score threshold.
- **Indexing everything**: irrelevant documents add noise and can poison retrieval. Be surgical about what goes in the knowledge base.
- **Never reindexing**: as documents update, chunks become stale. Implement incremental reindexing or TTLs on chunk freshness.

## Reflection questions

1. Explain why cosine similarity is used instead of Euclidean distance for comparing embeddings in most RAG implementations.
2. You have a 400-page technical manual. What chunk size and overlap would you choose, and why? What would you do differently for a 400-page novel?
3. Your retrieval recall is 72% but your end-to-end answer accuracy is only 45%. What might explain the gap?
4. Why does BM25 complement dense embeddings rather than being made obsolete by them?
5. A client asks: "Can we just embed the entire document as one big chunk?" How do you respond?
6. What is the difference between a cross-encoder and a bi-encoder, and why does it matter for reranking?
7. Design a faithfulness eval: given a model answer and the retrieved context, how would you measure whether the answer is grounded?

## My take (reviewer lens)

Karpathy would find most RAG systems embarrassingly unprincipled — built with no understanding of what the embedding actually encodes. He'd push you to visualize your embeddings (UMAP or t-SNE on a sample), look at what chunks are neighboring each other in embedding space, and actually understand whether the geometry of your embedding space matches the structure of your domain. Most people never look at their embeddings; they just trust the model.

Seibel would say: don't build GraphRAG in week one. Start with the naive RAG, measure what breaks, fix the most common failure first. Shipping a working 70%-quality RAG in a day beats architecting a perfect one that ships in a month. Your client's knowledge base is probably 100 documents. You do not need a knowledge graph.

Jason Liu (jxnl.co) would audit your evals first — he argues the most common failure is not that the RAG stack is wrong, but that the team has no feedback loop. You can't iterate on what you can't measure. Build the eval harness in day one, even before you build the retrieval pipeline.
