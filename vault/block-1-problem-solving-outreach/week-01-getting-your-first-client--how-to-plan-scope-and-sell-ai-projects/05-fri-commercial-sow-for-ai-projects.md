# The commercial SOW for AI projects — what's different, what's load-bearing, what will wreck you in 6 months

## Why this matters

A traditional software-services SOW ships a specification, a timeline, and a payment schedule. That document assumes the thing being built behaves deterministically once it is written: the login works, the report renders, the API returns 200. AI engagements violate that assumption before you leave the kickoff. What you are shipping has statistical behaviour, depends on upstream weights you do not own, drifts as soon as the traffic shifts, and costs real cash on every invocation. The contract you write for it therefore has to answer a different question than the old one. Not "what will we build?" — that is two paragraphs. The load-bearing question is "how will we both know it worked, and what happens when the floor shifts under us?"

By the end of this lesson you will be able to (1) name the six clauses that distinguish an AI SOW from a dev SOW and defend each to a procurement officer who wants to delete them, (2) write an eval harness as a contractual deliverable rather than as an internal engineering nicety, (3) allocate IP across base model / fine-tune / eval set / prompt library / customer data without either surrendering your methodology or overreaching on your client's data, (4) draft a token-cost pass-through formula that survives a 30% mid-project price swing in either direction without a renegotiation, (5) draft a model-drift SLA with specific metric, threshold, cadence, and remediation ladder — and know when you should refuse to sign one, (6) design termination clauses that handle the three AI-specific termination triggers your 2022 template does not cover: model-provider ToS/deprecation changes, eval-metric failure on a held-out set, and scope creep that is technical rather than commercial in origin. You will also have a defensible position on whether any pricing model (flat, milestone, outcome-based) actually survives AI-era economics, and you will know which clauses are fair-to-both and which you should never concede.

Block 0 Week 3 Wednesday taught the walking-skeleton scoping framework — how to carve a technical POC with kill criteria.[^1] Block 1 Thursday taught the phase structure for a commercial engagement — discovery → POC → pilot → production with exit ramps.[^2] This is the paper. The document your client signs. Read the two prior lessons if you have not; what follows assumes them.

## Prerequisites

- A working vocabulary for AI engagements: eval set, held-out data, drift, fine-tune, RAG, tokens, context window. If you can explain to a CFO why eval data and training data must be disjoint, you qualify.
- Experience with at least one non-AI services engagement — an SOW you wrote or signed — even if it was a marketing retainer or an engineering buildout. The lesson contrasts against that baseline.

## Layer 1 — What a dev SOW is for, and why an AI SOW is not that

A professional services SOW, stripped to its spine, exists to answer three questions with enough precision that a judge could read it three years later and adjudicate a dispute: *what work will be done, by when, for how much money*. The classic template defines scope, deliverables, timeline, acceptance criteria, payment schedule, and a handful of commercial boilerplate — warranty, confidentiality, termination, change orders, IP. Statement-of-work best-practice writing from contract firms like Sirion and Ironclad converges on this shape;[^3] it is the same shape whether you are building a React frontend or migrating a SAP module. The assumption baked into the form is that the *deliverable* is a discrete artifact with pass/fail acceptance — does the button exist, is the field populated, does the endpoint return the right schema.

An AI engagement breaks this assumption in four specific ways, and each break forces a clause that the 2022 template does not contain.

**Break 1 — The deliverable is a distribution, not a fact.** "The classifier ships at 92% F1 on the held-out eval" is a deliverable only if the eval set is defined, controlled, and versioned in the contract. Without that, the deliverable is a rhetorical aspiration. Ironclad's own AI-contract guidance notes that AI SLAs must move beyond uptime to include "model accuracy thresholds" and measurement methodology agreed at signing.[^4] In a dev SOW, "the button works" is self-verifying; in an AI SOW, "the model works" is a measurement protocol you either write down together or discover you disagreed about in month four.

**Break 2 — The artifact depends on upstream things you do not own.** A React component does not silently change behaviour when Facebook pushes a patch. A prompt-chained Claude agent does — when Anthropic ships a new model version, when context windows shift, when tool-calling semantics update. Anthropic's published deprecation policy commits to at least 60 days' notice for retired models, with formal lifecycle stages (active / legacy / deprecated / retired).[^5] OpenAI's May 2025 Services Agreement gives enterprise customers a termination right with 30-days notice if a material change reduces service functionality.[^6] These are not theoretical hooks. A system you designed against `gpt-4o` in March 2024 and a client deployed in June 2024 was running against a different evaluation baseline by the time the December 2024 Assistants v1 shutoff hit developers.[^7] Your SOW has to know this will happen and say what each side does when it does.

**Break 3 — Unit economics are a variable you both price against.** Traditional dev fees are labour-cost-plus. AI engagement costs have an additional variable cost — inference tokens — that neither party controls and that has moved by factors of three to ten in the last eighteen months across model tiers.[^8] A fixed fee on a high-volume LLM engagement can go underwater or massively over-quote the client depending on which direction the market moves. The "pass-through cost" clause from traditional services contracts (materials billed without markup) has a natural analogue here — but only if you build it explicitly.[^9]

**Break 4 — The IP question is not about the code.** In a dev SOW the IP question is roughly "who owns the source?" In an AI SOW there are at least five distinct IP surfaces — base model, model weights after fine-tuning on customer data, the customer's raw training corpus, the eval set you built, the prompt library you wrote, and the synthetic/derivative data generated during training. Morgan Lewis's September 2024 guidance lays out the canonical allocation — vendor keeps pre-trained weights and interface; customer keeps raw data; the improvements-to-base-model layer is the contested middle where most deals leak value.[^10] A contract that says "IP belongs to the client" without naming which of those five layers is either naive or predatory depending on which side wrote it.

The rest of this lesson walks each break and the clause that addresses it. The through-line: an AI SOW is a *governance document for a probabilistic system*, not an acceptance checklist for a deterministic one.

## Layer 2 — Eval harness as a contractual deliverable

This is the single most underpriced move in AI services. Most proposals treat evaluation as an internal engineering practice — the vendor runs some evals, the vendor says the model is ready, the client trusts or doesn't. That is the wrong structure. An AI SOW should name the eval harness *itself* as a deliverable, with the same weight as the model code, and should define three things about it: (a) the eval set composition and versioning, (b) the metric and threshold for each category of task, (c) the cadence at which the harness re-runs in production and who reads the output.

Hamel Husain's field-guide posts on AI consulting — the canonical operator writing on this topic — centre evals as the core artifact of every engagement.[^11] His summary of thirty-plus engagements is brutal in its simplicity: the teams that succeed "barely talk about tools at all, instead obsessing over measurement and iteration."[^12] Translating that to commercial contract language: if your SOW names the model but not the harness, you have written a contract that rewards demo performance and penalises production reality. The buyer will feel this in month three when the live-traffic behaviour diverges from the pilot; they will blame you, and the contract will not help either side resolve the dispute because nobody agreed in writing what "working" means.

**Concrete clause structure for eval-as-deliverable.** Three sub-clauses, borrowed from the Ironclad and Lexology guidance on AI contract clauses with operator additions:[^4][^13]

1. *Eval Set Definition and Control.* "Consultant will produce, and Client will jointly review and approve, an Eval Set of no fewer than [N] examples covering [categories], with at least [X]% held-out from all training and prompt-engineering activity. The Eval Set is a deliverable; Client will receive a versioned copy at each Phase Gate. Changes to the Eval Set composition require a Change Order."
2. *Metric and Threshold Specification.* "For each category, the Parties agree the measurement metric ([e.g., F1 for classification, LLM-judge rubric with named rubric document for open-generation, exact-match for extraction]) and the acceptance threshold. Threshold failures at Phase Gate trigger the remediation ladder in Section [X]."
3. *Ongoing Harness Operation.* "Consultant will deliver an Eval Harness capable of re-running the Eval Set against the production deployment on a [daily/weekly] cadence. Harness results are Client data; Consultant retains no right to use them for other engagements absent written consent."

The reason this is the highest-leverage clause in the whole document: it is the clause the vendor instinctively resists because it constrains them, and it is the clause the buyer's counsel does not know to insist on because it is technical rather than legal. When both sides understand why it matters, the negotiation becomes fast. When only one side understands, the side who understands takes all the slack. Be the side that understands.

**Cross-domain example — legal intake.** A mid-market law firm hiring you to ship an AI intake triage system could sign a contract that says "triage quality meets client standards." That is unenforceable. The eval-harness version says: "Eval Set of 400 real historical intakes spanning [practice-area categories]; LLM-judge rubric with five criteria (urgency correctly flagged, practice-area correctly routed, conflict-check trigger correctly raised, client-contactability correctly inferred, retention-pitch required flag correct); acceptance threshold: ≥85% on the composite, ≥95% on conflict-check trigger. Measurement cadence: weekly in production for 12 weeks, then monthly." Now both sides know what the argument is about, and the argument is smaller.

**Cross-domain example — finance ops variance commentary.** A CFO hiring you to build an LLM-assisted month-end variance commentary system gets a different harness: a held-out test set of 60 prior-month variance narratives written by the finance team itself, scored against by a three-criterion rubric (factual correctness against the ledger, inclusion of the three largest driver lines, appropriate hedging language for small absolute variances). Threshold: 80% pass rate on the composite, 0% tolerance on factual errors against the ledger. That last clause — 0% tolerance on factual errors — is a kill criterion, not a threshold, and should be named as such so remediation is distinct from the ongoing SLA work.

## Layer 3 — Data access and data-return clauses

Most AI engagements require the vendor to see data the client would not let a normal consultant near. That creates two clauses the dev SOW does not need: a data-access protocol and a data-return (or destruction) clause. The surface is complicated by regulatory overlay — GDPR, CCPA, India's DPDP Act, HIPAA where relevant — but the operator-level version is simpler than the lawyer-level version.

**Data access.** Name the specific categories of data the vendor will touch, the minimum-necessary principle that constrains that access, the storage locations and encryption posture, and the sub-processor list (the LLM provider counts; so does any vector DB, any logging service). OpenAI's own enterprise DPA is a reference document for what the full surface looks like;[^14] Anthropic's Zero Data Retention contract covers the equivalent surface for Claude-based engagements and requires a signed security addendum to activate.[^15] If the LLM you are using has a ZDR option and the client's data sensitivity warrants it, the SOW should either require ZDR or explicitly disclaim it so the client is not surprised later.

**Data return.** Not "we will delete everything" — that is hand-waving. The specific clause names three buckets: *raw customer data* (returned in a specified format within a specified window; 30 days post-termination is the SaaS-standard default), *derivative data* (embeddings, chunked corpora, synthetic-data generations — each named), and *training-artifacts that incorporate customer data* (which gets routed to the IP section below, because whether it is returned depends on who owns what). Morgan Lewis's February 2026 post on exit rights lists the specific export deliverables buyers should insist on: training/fine-tuning dataset exports, prompt libraries, evaluation datasets, performance logs, and workflow documentation.[^16] Each should be named in the SOW in advance, not invented during the transition.

**Operator rule:** If the client is regulated (finance, health, legal, education, public sector), the data-access and data-return clauses need a named operator on each side who will actually execute them. A provision that says "data will be securely deleted" without naming who at the vendor runs the deletion script and who at the client verifies it is theatre. Theatre collapses during the audit.

## Layer 4 — IP allocation across five surfaces

Here is where a good SOW earns its keep, because this is where the ambiguity has the highest dollar value. You must allocate rights across five surfaces, and each allocation is a real negotiation.

1. **Base model and model weights (vendor-side IP).** The provider of the foundation model (OpenAI, Anthropic, a fine-tune-able open model) owns this layer. Your SOW does not grant rights here; it acknowledges that the foundation-model provider's terms govern. Morgan Lewis's guidance is explicit: the vendor typically owns "the original model that was developed and trained prior to use of the customer's data (pre-trained), including the associated hyperparameters."[^10]
2. **Customer's raw data and derived outputs (customer-side IP).** The customer's data in, the model's outputs out — both stay with the customer by default in well-written AI contracts. OpenAI's enterprise terms, for example, "assign all copyright, patent, and other intellectual property rights in that output to [the customer]."[^17] Your SOW should mirror this: raw training data and inference outputs belong to the client.
3. **Fine-tune on customer data (the contested middle).** This is the surface most disputes happen on. A model fine-tuned on the customer's proprietary data is (a) an improvement to the vendor's base model that the vendor might want to reuse, and (b) an encoding of the customer's data that the customer will argue is their derivative. The clean allocations are three: *fully-client-owned fine-tune* (customer owns the weights; vendor cannot reuse; common for regulated or unique-data clients), *jointly-licensed* (client owns the weights; vendor has a license to use learnings in other engagements, typically excluding direct competitors — requires careful "competitor" definition), or *vendor-owned with client license* (vendor owns and can reuse; client has perpetual license for their own use; common when fine-tuning is productised and data is not especially sensitive). Morgan Lewis's structuring piece on this surface is the canonical reference.[^10] Whatever you choose, name it; do not leave it for later. And note the hard practical reality from the OpenAI enterprise terms: even if the contract says the customer owns the fine-tuning data, the base model *weights* after fine-tuning cannot be exported off OpenAI's infrastructure.[^17] Ownership-on-paper and portability-in-practice are not the same thing.
4. **Eval set and evaluation artifacts (negotiated).** The eval set is the customer's test of whether the work worked. Most customers want exclusive ownership of it. Most vendors want to retain the right to use the methodology on future engagements. The defensible compromise: customer owns the eval set itself (the examples, the rubric, the thresholds); vendor retains the right to use the *methodology* (the process for generating eval sets) and anonymised generalisations on future engagements.
5. **Prompt library and system instructions (negotiated, often contested).** The prompt library is the vendor's craft artefact. Saying "the client owns all prompts" at signing can wreck the vendor's ability to deliver this kind of work again. Saying "the vendor owns all prompts" at signing makes the client's future independence from the vendor artificially hard. The operator move: client owns the *deployed prompts specific to their application* (including any prompts that reference their schema, their taxonomies, their domain-specific terms); vendor retains the right to use *generic prompt patterns* (e.g., a chain-of-thought scaffold, a RAG answer-then-evaluate pattern) across other engagements.

Draft this section as a matrix in the SOW: one row per surface, three columns (who owns it / what rights the other side has / explicit carve-outs). A client's counsel can read that matrix in thirty seconds and either approve or counterpropose. A paragraph of prose on the same subject takes an hour and leaves room for silent misalignment.

## Layer 5 — Token-cost pass-through, and the fiction of the fixed fee

A flat fee on an AI engagement is a bet on inference economics holding still. They do not hold still. Model-tier pricing has moved by factors of three to ten across the 2023–2025 window; intermediate-tier costs have dropped by ~80% per token in some classes and inflated in others;[^8] 2026 is already showing divergence between flagship models (expensive, high-context) and specialised small models (cheap, fast). The fixed-fee pricing conversation is live across the consulting industry: McKinsey reports roughly 25% of its 2025 fees came from outcome-based contracts, and BCG's 2025 B2B pricing piece documents the accelerating pressure on time-and-materials models in AI-augmented work.[^18][^19] The operator-level question is narrower than "what pricing model wins?" — it is "how do we structure the contract so that neither side takes all the risk of the token curve moving?"

**Four viable structures, in order of operator preference for first-time engagements:**

1. *Fixed fee plus explicit pass-through.* The vendor charges a fixed fee for their labour and IP; inference token costs pass through to the client at cost, billed monthly on actuals, with an upper-bound monthly cap agreed at signing that triggers a conversation if exceeded. This is the Sirion-style pass-through cost clause adapted for tokens.[^9] The clause needs: a specific definition of covered costs (inference tokens on the named model tier(s), not embedding or vector DB or observability costs unless listed), a billing cadence, a reporting format (actual token counts, actual dollar cost, reconciled against the provider's invoice), and a cap with a renegotiation trigger.
2. *Fixed fee with banded scope on tokens.* The vendor quotes a fixed fee assuming inference volume in a named range (e.g., ≤50M tokens/month on the specified tier). Above-band usage triggers a tiered pricing adder; a 30%+ price move on the underlying model in either direction triggers a scope conversation. Simpler to administer; less fair if the model price moves a lot in one direction.
3. *Milestone-gated with phase-level re-pricing.* Each phase (discovery / POC / pilot / production — see Thursday's lesson[^2]) is priced separately, and subsequent phases can be re-priced based on the inference-economics picture at the phase gate. This is how most well-structured engagements I have seen actually work, because it aligns with the commercial phase structure you are already using.
4. *Outcome-based with a token cap.* The vendor is paid on the measurable outcome (eval metric delta, revenue impact, cost saved), with the vendor's delivery infrastructure including a committed token budget up to a cap. Morgan Lewis and McKinsey both flag this model as increasingly common but structurally difficult — "outcome-based models transfer more risk to the vendor and align cost directly with value delivered, though they are complex to structure and often involve longer sales cycles."[^19] For first engagements, skip this. For your third or fourth with a given client where you have baseline data on their system, it can be the right call.

Jonathan Stark's *Hourly Billing Is Nuts* framing is often read as "fixed beats hourly, always."[^20] Stark's own caveat is load-bearing and often ignored: he wouldn't take work that is too risky to estimate within an order of magnitude, because "if you can't tell a client within that range how much it will cost them, you probably shouldn't take on that client."[^21] AI engagements with volatile token economics often fail that test. The pass-through clause is how you keep the fixed-fee structure while refusing to bet your margin on a pricing curve neither of you controls.

**Patio11's live pushback.** The "fixed fee plus pass-through" preference ordering above is not uncontested operator doctrine. Patrick McKenzie's (patio11) consulting writing argues the stronger move for an independent is to *"name one big number, and make it boring"* — a single flat fee priced high enough that token variance is absorbed inside margin, with no separate pass-through line item.[^22][^28] His reasoning: a pass-through clause leaks simplicity, invites procurement to audit monthly invoices line by line, and re-opens every month a pricing conversation the fixed fee was supposed to close. First-time readers should treat "fixed fee + pass-through" as the default for *forecastable-volume* engagements only (variance within roughly ±20% of the quoted volume), and should treat the patio11 all-in number as the correct structure when volume is forecastable at all; the hybrid becomes load-bearing precisely when the volume cannot be forecast within a margin band the flat fee can absorb.

**Live controversy (Position A vs Position B).** *Position A — flat fee is the only honest structure for a first engagement.* Jonathan Stark and Patrick McKenzie both argue, in different vocabulary, that hourly and milestone billing misalign incentives and leak value;[^20][^22] a clean flat fee forces the vendor to estimate honestly and the client to commit honestly. *Position B — no flat fee survives a 30% token-cost swing without mispricing one side.* Operators running high-volume LLM engagements in 2024–2025 point to their own margin compressions (or windfalls) from model-price moves that no one forecasted. The honest resolution: flat fee on the labour-and-IP layer, pass-through on the token layer, phase-gated re-pricing at the seam. That hybrid survives both arguments' evidence.

## Layer 6 — The model-drift SLA (and when to refuse one)

Drift is the AI-specific availability concept. A model that passes eval on day one and fails by month four has not broken in the classical sense — no line of code changed — but the thing you deployed is no longer the thing you contracted for. The SLA clause for this is different from the availability SLA you know from SaaS (99.9% uptime).

**The minimum viable drift SLA has four components:** a named metric, a threshold, a measurement cadence, and a remediation ladder. Triumph Law's San Mateo AI-MSA guidance and the Lexology AI-contract round-up converge on this shape.[^13] Industry practice on thresholds from the drift-detection literature: monthly degradation limits commonly set between 0.5% and 2%, with accuracy floors specified as a minimum acceptable rate (e.g., 85–95%) depending on task criticality.[^23]

A concrete example clause, adapted from the Lexology and CCSD Council templates[^13][^24] and simplified for operator use:

> *"Drift Monitoring. The Eval Harness delivered under Section [X] will run the Eval Set against the production deployment on a [weekly] cadence. The 'Primary Metric' is [e.g., composite LLM-judge rubric score] with acceptance threshold [T] (as a worked default for a classification-style system: T = 0.88 composite F1, using the Terms.Law-documented 85–95% accuracy-floor band[^23] as the negotiating anchor). The 'Drift Trigger' is either (a) a rolling four-week average below T, or (b) a single-week drop exceeding [Δ] from the rolling four-week mean, where Δ defaults to the Terms.Law-documented 0.5–2% monthly-degradation band — i.e. Δ = 2 percentage points absolute as the permissive threshold, 0.5 points as the strict threshold for high-criticality tasks (regulated-industry or compliance-bearing workflows).[^23] On Drift Trigger, Consultant will, within [N] business days, diagnose the cause (model-provider change, data-distribution shift, prompt/retrieval regression, integration change) and either (i) restore the metric via reasonable effort at no additional fee, or (ii) if the cause is material Client-side data distribution change not reasonably attributable to Consultant's work, propose a Change Order."*

The remediation ladder matters. The Lexology and Morgan Lewis guidance both emphasise that remedies must extend beyond refunds: "cooperation in mitigation, legal defense support, or notifications to affected users."[^13][^16] The operator version: first rung is diagnosis + free-remediation attempt, second rung is Change Order with re-scoped thresholds, third rung is Client-side termination right with pre-agreed transition assistance.

**When to refuse a drift SLA.** There are cases where the honest move is to decline. If the use case is inherently non-stationary — for example, a classifier on adversarial user-generated content where the distribution shifts weekly by design — the drift SLA becomes a ratchet that forces you to overfit to yesterday's data. If the client's data infrastructure has no monitoring (they cannot tell you what their current input distribution looks like), you cannot commit to a drift SLA because you cannot measure the drift source. If the client refuses to agree on the eval set, you cannot commit to drift on an undefined metric. In each of these cases, refuse the SLA in writing and specify a monitoring-and-notification commitment instead: you will measure and report, and drift events will trigger a conversation, not a contractual liability. That is the fair commitment when the preconditions for an SLA are not met.

## Layer 7 — Termination clauses for the three AI-specific triggers

A 2022 dev-SOW termination clause covers: termination for convenience (with notice), termination for cause (with cure period), termination for non-payment, and termination for change-of-control. An AI SOW needs three additions — or the termination right is missing exactly when you need it.

**Trigger 1 — Model-provider ToS / deprecation change.** OpenAI's Services Agreement (effective May 31, 2025) grants enterprise customers termination-for-convenience on 30-days notice when an update "materially reduces the Services functionality."[^6] Anthropic's model deprecation policy commits to 60-days notice for model retirement.[^5] Translate these up to your client's SOW: if your delivery depends on a named foundation-model SKU and that SKU's terms, data retention, deprecation, or pricing materially change, the client gets a termination-for-convenience right with a pro-rated refund and transition assistance; so does the vendor (so that you are not contractually obliged to deliver on a model that no longer exists). In October 2025, clients of multiple AI vendors walked after OpenAI changed enterprise data-retention defaults under a federal preservation order — a court-ordered indefinite retention obligation that was clarified to exclude ChatGPT Enterprise but broke the contractual assumptions of companies relying on consumer-tier endpoints, before obligations wound down in late September 2025.[^25] If those clients' SOWs had not contained a ToS-change termination clause, the walk would have been a dispute; because some did, the walk was just an administrative transition.

**Trigger 2 — Eval-metric failure on the held-out set after [N] remediation cycles.** The drift SLA handles post-launch; this clause handles pre-launch. If the eval-harness composite cannot reach the acceptance threshold at the Phase Gate after two or three remediation cycles, either side can terminate with pre-agreed exit consideration. This is the "soft exit" at the end of POC that Block 1 Thursday flagged as a problem-set exercise.[^2] Do not omit it — the "pilot trap" where a POC drifts indefinitely because neither side wants to admit it failed is almost always a missing or vague eval-failure termination clause.

**Trigger 3 — Scope creep driven by the eval rather than by the commercial.** Ordinary scope creep is "the client wants more features." AI-project scope creep is often "the eval revealed that the problem was actually different from what we thought, and solving the actual problem requires different data / different evaluation / different model family." That is technical scope creep, and the SOW should have a Change Order trigger for it, not a termination clause — but the Change Order clause should name this specific kind of pivot. Traditional Change Order templates assume feature-level additions; the AI version needs to handle category-of-problem-level redefinition.[^26] If you cannot agree a Change Order within [N] weeks after the trigger, the SOW should default to a termination-for-convenience right on either side with pre-agreed exit consideration.

## Layer 8 — Change orders that do not poison the engagement

The Change Order clause is the third most-litigated surface of any services engagement after scope and IP; in AI engagements, it is more often-triggered because the technical surface genuinely shifts during the work. Two rules from the operator literature keep the clause from becoming a source of resentment.

**Rule 1 — Name the "out of scope" categories explicitly at signing.** Contract Nerds' SOW best-practice guidance is clear: most scope creep happens because SOWs fail to state what is *out* of scope, not because they fail to state what is in.[^27] For AI engagements, explicit out-of-scope categories I recommend naming: additional data sources beyond those listed in the Data Access section, additional languages beyond the contracted set, additional integration surfaces beyond those named, change of foundation-model family (e.g., GPT-class → Claude-class), change of deployment target (e.g., cloud → on-prem), addition of safety/compliance features not scoped at signing.

**Rule 2 — Pre-price the change-order rate card at signing.** Consulting Quest's research on change orders and scope creep names the two failure modes directly: "scope creep eats margin" and "buyer resents constant change orders."[^26] Both fail modes come from negotiating change-order pricing in the middle of the project, when the parties are already under stress. The operator fix: at signing, include a change-order rate card (e.g., "change orders will be priced at $[X]/day of additional work for engineering, $[Y]/day for eval redesign, $[Z]/day for infrastructure; minimum change-order size [$K]"). Now the conversation at trigger-time is about whether a change is warranted, not about what it costs.

## Operator war stories

**Story 1 — The OpenAI retention policy shift (2025).** In May 2025, a federal magistrate ordered OpenAI to preserve all ChatGPT user conversations indefinitely — including those users had explicitly deleted — as part of discovery in a data-demands dispute. The court clarified on May 27 that ChatGPT Enterprise was excluded from the preservation order, but consumer and API-tier customers were affected until the obligation wound down on September 26, 2025.[^25] For AI consultants who had architected client systems against the consumer API with the pre-order privacy expectations, the clients who had SOWs with a ToS-change termination clause (like the one in Layer 7 above) executed clean 30-day walks. The clients without such clauses had lawyer-intensive renegotiations. The lesson is not "always clause against ToS changes" — it is that in AI engagements, a ToS change *will* happen within a 12-month window with some non-trivial probability, and pricing that in contractually at signing is an order of magnitude cheaper than pricing it in via counsel at trigger time.

**Story 2 — The patio11 rate-card thread and the E&O insurer.** Patrick McKenzie (patio11) has publicly documented, across his Kalzumeus writing and X threads, that an MSA + SOW structure serves a non-obvious second purpose: it gives the consultant's Errors & Omissions insurer something concrete to adjudicate against if a claim is made.[^28] His engagement range has been documented at $8K–$50K per week across his active consulting period.[^22] The operator insight that matters here: an AI engagement without a written SOW describing deliverables and acceptance criteria is an insurance-uncovered engagement. If your client ships the AI you built, the model drifts, a regulatory or user harm results, and the client sues, your E&O cover will be stronger with a clear eval-threshold deliverable in the SOW than with "we agreed I would build some AI for them." Treat the SOW as the document that keeps your insurer on-side, not just the document that gets you paid.

**Story 3 — The Harvey platform termination cadence.** Harvey's standard platform agreement includes a mutual termination-for-convenience clause and a 30-day cure period for material breach before termination-for-cause.[^29] This is conservative boilerplate for a buyer's-market enterprise AI sale. The instructive part for an independent AI consultant: if you are selling *into* a law firm like Reed Smith that already uses Harvey, your SOW has to clear a procurement bar that has been calibrated by prior AI-vendor contracts. The client's counsel will compare your IP, drift, and termination clauses to the Harvey baseline. Come in with a tighter, better-specified SOW than Harvey's — especially on the eval-harness and drift clauses, which SaaS-style agreements tend to leave to the SLA document — and you will close faster. Come in with generic dev-SOW language and your proposal will route through a legal review that adds four weeks.

## Runnable experiment

**Phase 1 — Generate the baseline SOW.** Ask Claude Code to draft a commercial SOW for a specific AI engagement you are considering (or a hypothetical one with realistic parameters — e.g., intake-triage agent for a 40-lawyer mid-market firm, 12-week engagement, expected ~80M input tokens/month in production). Prompt structure:

> *"Draft a complete commercial SOW for the following AI engagement: [describe]. The SOW must include, as distinct clauses: (a) Phase-gated deliverables with eval-harness as a named deliverable for each phase, (b) Data access clauses naming specific data categories, sub-processors, and minimum-necessary scope, (c) Data return clause covering raw, derivative, and training-artifact buckets with 30-day post-termination window, (d) IP allocation matrix across five surfaces: base model, customer raw data, fine-tune weights, eval set, prompt library — one row per surface, three columns (owner / other-side rights / carve-outs), (e) Token-cost pass-through formula with monthly cap and 30% price-swing trigger, (f) Model-drift SLA with named metric, threshold, cadence, remediation ladder, (g) Termination clauses for (i) model-provider ToS/deprecation material change, (ii) eval-metric failure after two remediation cycles, (iii) scope creep requiring category-level redefinition, (h) Change order process with pre-priced rate card and named out-of-scope categories. Include specific dollar placeholders and specific numeric thresholds; do not leave things as 'TBD'."*

**Phase 2 — Stress-test from the buyer's counsel side.** Open Claude.ai in a fresh conversation. Paste the generated SOW. Prompt:

> *"You are the outside counsel for the [law firm / CFO office / product VP] buyer. Rewrite each clause to favour the buyer without making the contract unsignable from the vendor's side. Flag the three clauses you would push back on hardest and why. Flag any clauses where the vendor has written something a reasonable buyer should never concede."*

**Phase 3 — Reconcile.** Write a 500-word memo in your own voice naming: (1) which of the buyer-counsel-side rewrites you should accept, (2) which you should negotiate against and what your counter-position is, (3) which are "bright-line" clauses you would walk from the deal before conceding (typical candidates: eval-harness as a deliverable, pass-through on token cost, a drift-SLA with kill criteria rather than only credits, some form of ToS-change termination right). Defend each position with one sentence of reasoning that a non-lawyer buyer would accept.

The point of this experiment is not to produce a single SOW. It is to give you the feel of how the document moves under adversarial review so that by the time a real client's counsel sends their redlines, you know which of your positions are load-bearing and which are negotiating-room.

## Problem set

1. **IP clause for fine-tuning on client data.** Write the IP-allocation clause for a project that fine-tunes an open-weights model on a client's proprietary customer-service transcripts. Defend, in 150 words, which of the three Morgan Lewis allocations (fully-client-owned, jointly-licensed, vendor-owned-with-client-license) you chose and why. Name the two carve-outs your client's counsel will most likely push for and which of those you would accept.

2. **Token-cost pass-through formula.** Propose a specific pass-through formula for an engagement expected to use ~80M input + ~20M output tokens per month on a named model tier. Your formula must protect both sides from a 30% price swing in either direction without requiring renegotiation for swings under that threshold. State the monthly cap that triggers a conversation and the maximum cap before termination rights engage.

3. **Drift SLA.** Draft a model-drift SLA for an AI engagement of your choice (classification, generation, extraction — pick one). Name the specific eval metric, the threshold, the measurement cadence, the drift-trigger definition (rolling average vs single-week drop), and the three-rung remediation ladder. Add one sentence naming the condition under which you would refuse to sign a drift SLA at all.

4. **Change-order trigger design.** Design a Change Order trigger specifically for the "eval-revealed-wrong-problem" scope creep case. The trigger should avoid both fail modes: "scope creep eats margin" and "buyer resents constant change orders." Name the specific observation that activates the trigger, the change-order rate-card structure you would include at signing, and the fall-through termination clause if a Change Order cannot be agreed.

5. **Defensible position — ToS-change termination.** Take and defend, in 200 words, a position on the claim: *"Every AI SOW in 2026 should include a termination clause specifically triggered by material changes to the foundation-model provider's Terms of Service or data-retention policy."* Cite at least one specific documented event in 2024–2025 where such a clause would have mattered. Acknowledge the strongest counter-argument and say why it does not change your position.

## Common failure modes at scale

- **"IP belongs to the client" without specifying which layer.** The single most common drafting error. The client's counsel pastes their standard works-for-hire IP clause; the vendor does not push back because they are not sure where to push back. Six months later the vendor cannot reuse a prompt pattern they invented because the SOW says "all work product" is the client's. Push back at signing with the five-surface matrix; it reads as professional, not predatory.
- **Flat fee with no pass-through, signed in a stable-pricing quarter.** Works great until the model-price move. Operators report margin compression of 30–50% on high-volume engagements priced in Q3 2024 and executed through Q1 2025 due to token-price moves no one forecasted (both up and down, depending on model tier and provider).
- **Drift SLA with only service credits as remediation.** A 5% service credit on a $40K engagement does not pay for the engineering work of diagnosing drift and re-training. Ladder the remediation: diagnosis-plus-free-fix first, credit second, termination right third. The client wants the problem fixed, not the credit.
- **No named eval-set at signing.** The eval set gets invented during the engagement, which means the acceptance threshold also gets invented during the engagement, which means every acceptance conversation is a negotiation. Name the eval-set composition, size, and rubric in the SOW or as a reference schedule attached to it.
- **Termination clauses that ignore ToS changes.** Surveyed SOWs from 2022 dev-services templates do not have model-provider ToS-change termination clauses because they did not need them. Copying these templates for AI engagements inherits the gap.

## Open questions / what's not settled

1. *Does outcome-based pricing survive as AI-services standard, or is the pass-through-fixed-fee hybrid the stable attractor?* McKinsey's 25% outcome-based fee share as of 2025 is increasing slowly;[^19] BCG's B2B pricing reset piece argues the category is genuinely moving.[^18] Operators on the ground mostly report that fixed-fee + pass-through is where deals actually settle. Watch the 2026 data.
2. *Who owns a fine-tune trained on public data the vendor scraped plus the client's data?* Morgan Lewis's structuring piece touches this edge case; the copyright-and-AI follow-up from September 2025 revisits it.[^10][^30] The settled operator position is "avoid the case by making the public-data layer explicit in the data-access clause" — i.e., push the question out of the IP section and into the data section — but this is a workaround, not a resolution.
3. *Do drift SLAs raise or lower vendor liability exposure overall?* A named drift SLA creates a specific contractual commitment, which is a liability surface. The counter-argument is that without one, the ordinary-negligence standard in a dispute is more vendor-hostile than the named threshold. Counsel on both sides disagree; the empirical data on disputes does not exist publicly.

## Reviewer lens — named critics with specific disagreements

- **Patrick McKenzie (patio11), Kalzumeus archive and X threads on consulting contracts.**[^22][^28] patio11 would likely push back on Layer 5's preference ordering of pricing structures — specifically the ranking of "fixed fee plus explicit pass-through" as #1 for first engagements. His consulting writing repeatedly argues the strongest move for an independent consultant working with enterprise buyers is "name one big number, and make it boring" — fixed everything, priced high enough that the token variance is inside your margin. His case: the pass-through clause leaks simplicity and invites procurement to audit monthly invoices. My response: for engagements where token volume is forecastable within ±20% he is right and the flat fee is cleaner; for high-volume or long-horizon engagements the variance exceeds any reasonable margin buffer and the pass-through becomes load-bearing. Disagreement is about where the volume threshold sits, not about the underlying mechanics.

- **Jonathan Stark, *Hourly Billing Is Nuts* and the Ditching Hourly podcast.**[^20][^21] Stark would push back on the entire framing of Layer 5 for a different reason: that the "pricing structure" debate is downstream of the "are we doing value pricing at all" debate, and any discussion that treats cost as the base and scales from there has already lost the plot. His position: price against the *client's perceived value* of the outcome, not against the vendor's expected cost. The lesson's concession is that outcome-based pricing is appropriate for later-stage engagements once the vendor has baseline data; Stark would argue that concession is a rationalisation, and first engagements are where value pricing pays the most because you have the most pricing power at the "new relationship" moment. The honest resolution: Stark is right for a specific class of engagements (well-understood outcomes, quantifiable value) and the lesson's framing is right for another (novel AI work with genuinely uncertain outcomes). Name the class before you choose the structure.

- **Hamel Husain, parlance-labs field-guide posts and the AI Evals Maven course.**[^11][^12] Hamel would specifically push on Layer 2's framing of eval-harness-as-deliverable. His stronger claim is that the eval harness is not one deliverable among several — it is the *only* deliverable that matters, and everything else in the SOW (including the model itself) is a means to pass the harness. His practical case from 30+ engagements is that the teams that succeed obsess over measurement; the teams that fail talk about tooling. The lesson treats the harness as co-equal with model and prompt IP surfaces; Hamel's stronger position is that without the harness, the rest is unverifiable and therefore commercially irrelevant. I accept this as a framing upgrade: the eval harness is the contractual centre of gravity, and the other clauses exist to protect the parties' ability to operate around it.

- **Morgan Lewis Tech & Sourcing blog, Traub and Archer on exit rights (Feb 2026), Lamb and others on IP allocation (2024).**[^10][^16] The Morgan Lewis team's specific disagreement with Layer 4 would be that the five-surface IP matrix is insufficient — their 2026 post identifies exit rights and portability as a sixth surface that needs its own clause, and the lesson folds that into the Data Return clause rather than splitting it out. Their argument: portability deliverables (prompt library exports, fine-tuned-model export where possible, eval-set exports, workflow documentation) are commercially different from raw-data return and should be negotiated separately. Partial concession: for engagements where vendor lock-in is a material risk (healthcare, regulated industries, any deal where switching costs would exceed 20% of the original deal size), split portability into its own clause; for smaller engagements, folding it into Data Return is operationally simpler without meaningful loss.

- **Lexology "Eight essential clauses for AI contracts" guide (2025) and Triumph Law AI-MSA guidance.**[^13] The Lexology authors would push back on Layer 6's recommendation to refuse a drift SLA in non-stationary cases. Their position is that the drift SLA should be written such that the threshold *itself* can be revised on a cadence (e.g., quarterly threshold re-basing against trailing production performance), so that adversarial-distribution cases do not break the SLA — the SLA becomes self-calibrating. This is a more sophisticated drafting pattern than the one Layer 6 recommends. I accept the pushback: for vendors with the drafting sophistication to write a self-calibrating SLA, that is superior to refusal; for first-time AI-services vendors, the simpler "monitoring-and-notification commitment" is more defensible in negotiation. Both positions belong in an expert's toolbox.
