# Building in public for AI consultants — case studies, receipts, and whether content is actually a moat

## Why this matters

Somewhere in the second year of an AI-services practice there is a fork. A consultant starts to notice that the people who closed the last three retainers each mentioned, unprompted, a specific artifact: a case-study teardown, a revenue-disclosure thread, a before-and-after screenshot posted months ago. The marketing budget was zero. The pipeline came from *receipts* — work made visible in public, in a form that a skeptical buyer could audit. At the same fork, the consultant reads a Pieter Levels thread saying that content is not a moat, that content is a faucet you have to keep running or it turns off, and that the real business is the product. Both things appear to be true at the same time, said by credible operators with real numbers.[^1][^2] This is the day you develop a defensible position on that contradiction, because your next twelve months of publishing depend on it.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to: (1) operationalize the "receipts shelf" as a deliberate artifact portfolio — five to seven objects whose combined effect collapses a skeptical buyer's diligence cycle from weeks to a single evening, (2) write a case study in the three working formats (narrative, receipts-heavy, teardown) and pick the right one for the buyer you actually want, (3) hold a defensible position in the content-as-moat debate — understanding the Arvid Kahl "compounding asset" thesis, the Pieter Levels "faucet" counter, and the Patrick McKenzie evergreen-asset synthesis that sits between them,[^3][^4][^12] (4) recognize the creator-consultant cognitive-dissonance trap — the revenue-mix ratio at which the content itself becomes the product and quietly replaces the service it was supposed to sell, and (5) publish a named-operator revenue disclosure without the two failure modes: vanity numbers (no context) and humble-brag concealment (numbers without the mechanism that produced them). This is not a creator-economy lesson. It is a commercial-substrate lesson for operators whose output is sold work, not whose output is attention.

## Prerequisites

- You have finished Monday (three-pillar brand model: proof, taste, opinion) and Tuesday (platform and cadence choice) of this week. If you have not, the receipts-shelf concept here will float free of the theory that grounds it. Monday's "proof pillar" is what this lesson operationalizes into artifacts.
- You have shipped at least one AI engagement — client, internal, or side project — that produced a visible outcome. If you have not, you do not yet have receipts; you have intentions. Hold this lesson for the end of your first engagement.
- Optional: have a look at Sahil Lavingia's public Gumroad financial disclosures on X and Jon Yongfook's Bannerbear Open Startup page before reading further. You will see the shape that named-operator transparency takes in its mature form.[^5][^6]

## Layer 1 — The receipts shelf, operationalized

Monday introduced proof as one of three brand pillars. Most operators stop at that level of abstraction and publish ad-hoc "case studies" when the mood or the pipeline demands it. The receipts-shelf discipline is different: it is a deliberate, maintained portfolio of five to seven artifacts, each engineered to answer a specific diligence question a buyer would otherwise need a reference call to answer. The shelf is not an archive of past posts. It is the set of objects a buyer can find in twenty minutes of Googling your name that does the work of a forty-five-minute reference interview.

The seven slots, in priority order:

**Slot 1 — one flagship case study with numbers.** One engagement, fully disclosed within what the NDA permits: problem framing, approach, before/after metric, one honest thing that went wrong. Hamel Husain's April 2025 *Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products* functions as this artifact; it names specific client engagements — Rechat (real-estate CRM) and Nurture Boss (apartment-industry assistant where "the AI was failing 66% of the time when users said things like 'let's schedule a tour two weeks from now'") — and uses them to demonstrate the eval-driven method he sells.[^7] Buyers read the number, understand the mechanism that produced the improvement, and can immediately picture their own problem being handled the same way. The piece is also a credential-manufacturing machine: after publication, Husain and Shreya Shankar's *AI Evals for Engineers and PMs* course on Maven hit over 3,000 students from 500+ companies including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google.[^7][^8] One case study produced both a defensible positioning and a distribution engine.

**Slot 2 — a public-facing teardown of a failure.** A post that analyzes a project — yours or a public one — that *didn't work*, and explains the mechanism of failure. This is counter-intuitive and load-bearing. A buyer deciding to give you $50,000 of their budget is not hiring your wins; they are hiring your *taste for which projects to kill*. A teardown of a failure — your own, with the discipline to attribute correctly — is stronger proof of judgment than three success stories. Most operators refuse to publish these, which is why the ones who do get disproportionate signal.

**Slot 3 — a working artifact the reader can use.** A prompt, a rubric, a small utility, an eval harness, a Figma file, a template — something that has real utility *without buying anything from you*. The rule: the artifact must be usable by a peer, not a marketing asset disguised as a resource. If it functions as a gated email capture, it is Slot 7 (audience-building), not Slot 3 (proof).

**Slot 4 — a live point of view on a specific live debate.** One essay or thread where you stake a specific position on a contested question — not "AI will change everything," but "the eval-first methodology systematically underweights rare-event failure modes in voice-agent interfaces" or "flat-fee AI consulting can't survive a 30% token-price swing without a margin-protection clause." Monday's "opinion pillar" gets its proof here.

**Slot 5 — a visible track of iteration over time.** A blog, newsletter, or feed where a buyer can see twelve or more months of consistent thinking, not a dump of three posts from last week. This is where the *compounding* thesis lives or dies. Patrick McKenzie's `kalzumeus.com/greatest-hits/` page has been doing this for over a decade — fifteen years of evergreen writing indexed for diligence, and "a 10-year-old blog post outranking the product site" is, as he has observed, a consistent anti-pattern that he turned into an asset.[^12]

**Slot 6 — a peer or client reference that is searchable.** A quote, a shared panel, a public conversation where a named operator the buyer respects associates themselves with your work. This is not a LinkedIn recommendation. It is Ethan Mollick retweeting your eval post, or Teresa Torres quoting your discovery framework on a podcast, or a client CTO speaking at a conference with your name on the slide. It is earned, not requested.

**Slot 7 — a distribution channel of your own.** A newsletter, podcast, or long-running social feed large enough that you can get a message to more than a thousand relevant readers without paying for it. This matters for reasons Layer 3 will develop — it is what converts the other six slots into pipeline.

Notice what's not on this list: testimonials, client logos, a services page with bullet points. Those are table stakes; they do not move the diligence needle in an asymmetric-information market the way the seven slots above do. A mature receipts shelf does not replace your services page; it makes the services page decorative rather than load-bearing.

## Layer 2 — Case study mechanics: three formats and the buyer each one attracts

A case study is the highest-leverage artifact on the shelf, and most of them are written to the wrong spec. The correction is small and mechanical, and it's a *buyer-segmentation* decision before it's a format decision — April Dunford's "best-fit customer" component from *Obviously Awesome* applied to content: pick the one buyer whose context, vocabulary, and diligence habits should determine everything else on the page, then let the format fall out of that choice. Match the *format* of the case study to the *buyer* you want, not to the engagement you had. Three formats, three distinct buyer profiles, and each one is a different positioning consequence.

**Format A — the narrative case study.** Story arc, minimal numbers, emphasis on diagnosis and decision. "The CMO had a $300K budget for an AI-enabled campaign and a board meeting in five weeks. Here's what we found in week one that changed what we built." This format attracts operator-buyers and mid-market CMOs, CTOs, heads of ops. They read it like a novella and recognize themselves in the problem. They will call you because they trust you with ambiguity. Weakness: heavy narrative makes it easy for a procurement-driven buyer to dismiss as "anecdote" when comparing you to a larger firm's deck.

**Format B — the receipts-heavy case study.** Numbers first, minimal story. "Baseline support-response time: 14.3 hours. Final: 1.1 hours. Eval pass-rate: 42% → 87% on a 500-example held-out set. Cost per ticket: $3.40 → $0.62. Timeline: 9 weeks." This format attracts financial buyers — CFOs, VPs of Finance, procurement-led evaluations, and board-reviewed spend. They screen hundreds of pitches and need to classify yours in thirty seconds. They will call you because the numbers survive comparison to the McKinsey deck. Weakness: numbers without mechanism read as either suspicious or commoditized, and the buyer who wants to understand *how* will skip you.

**Format C — the teardown case study.** Before/after artifact, mechanism explanation, code or rubric shown. "Here's the prompt we started with. Here's the eval that caught it failing on tool-name disambiguation in 23% of cases. Here's the retrieval-plus-structured-prompt fix. Here's the eval score after. Here's what still doesn't work." This format attracts technical buyers — VPs of Engineering, heads of AI, product-led orgs. They will call you because you demonstrated, at the level of *mechanism*, that you understand their problem class. Weakness: bleeds information that competitors can copy; alienates non-technical buyers who cannot evaluate the mechanism.

Matching format to buyer requires you to pick the buyer first, not the other way around. The most common failure mode is a consultant writing a Format-C teardown because it's the most *defensible* form, then being disappointed that no CFOs call. Format C attracts Format-C buyers. If you want a seven-figure enterprise retainer paid by a CFO, write Format B — even if you personally find Format C more satisfying to produce.

The secondary failure: treating these as mutually exclusive. Husain's *Field Guide* is all three simultaneously — narrative arc (he weaves client engagements into a problem-diagnosis sequence), receipts (the 66% failure-rate number, client names, specific benchmark deltas), and teardown (code listings, rubric explanations, LLM-as-judge mechanics).[^7] This is why the piece compounds. Most operators cannot sustain a fully-fused multi-format artifact more than once or twice a year. That is fine — the shelf has seven slots exactly because no single artifact carries all the load.

## Layer 3 — Content-as-moat: the debate, with both sides steelmanned

Now the controversy the brief mandates. Three named positions, held by credible operators with real revenue attached to their argument.

**Position A — content is a compounding asset (Arvid Kahl; Patrick McKenzie).** Evergreen content — essays, tutorials, rubrics, case studies — accrues value over time through three mechanisms: search-engine half-life, citation-compounding (other writers link to strong posts, increasing authority), and identity-compounding (a ten-year consistent body of work signals taste and stamina in a way a twelve-month body cannot). Patrick McKenzie's career is the canonical instance: years of evergreen writing at kalzumeus.com functioned as a persistent sales engine that moved his consulting rate from $100/hour to $30,000/week and eventually to a senior role at Stripe and an ongoing newsletter at bitsaboutmoney.com.[^12][^13] The "10-year-old blog post outranks my product site" pattern he documented is not an anti-pattern for a consultant; it is the entire mechanism of the compound. Arvid Kahl has built a similar shelf at thebootstrappedfounder.com — podcast, newsletter, books, YouTube — and has run it consistently enough that Podscan's "long-term investments are compounding, from programmatic SEO earning backlinks from major publications to an OP3 integration improving data fidelity."[^3] The content is both the distribution for Podscan *and* an asset independent of any specific product.

**Position B — content is a faucet, not a moat (Pieter Levels).** The counter, stated by Levels across years of X posts and interviews, is that content is a tap you have to keep running. Stop posting, and within a quarter the traffic and sign-ups decline. Content is not a moat because it does not compound in a way that prevents a competitor from standing up their own faucet next quarter; moats are network effects, switching costs, proprietary data, or brand-as-default — not posts.[^1][^2] Levels backs this with receipts of his own: PhotoAI reached $100K/month in revenue in September 2024, about eighteen months after launch, and was at approximately $132-138K MRR by November 2025 with an annual run rate of $1.6-1.65M.[^1] Interior AI was at ~$38-45K MRR with >99% profit margins ("GPU bill is minimal, run by AI robots with no people," as he put it in a 2024 Hacker News thread).[^2] The growth engine was *product and distribution*, not a compounding content library. In Levels' framing, the content gets attention; the product converts it; the moat is in the product economics.

**Position C — content is an evergreen asset, not a moat (Patrick McKenzie's narrower claim).** McKenzie's actual position, read carefully, is more precise than Position A: evergreen content — writing that answers a perpetually recurring question a customer has, not writing that reports on current events — is an asset on the balance sheet of a consulting practice.[^12] Asset, not moat. A moat prevents competitors from catching you; an asset generates cash flow. McKenzie has never claimed evergreen writing is a moat; he has claimed it is a durable, high-ROI asset compared to the alternative of no writing. This distinction is the reconciliation between Positions A and B.

**My position:** Position C is correct; Positions A and B are each half of it. Content is not a moat for AI services — the moat is client outcomes, retention, and reference-network density. But evergreen content is a high-ROI *asset* that pays for itself over a multi-year horizon if you write to answer perpetually recurring questions in your niche, and it does not pay if you write to participate in the attention cycle. Levels is right that a faucet you turn off stops flowing; he is describing non-evergreen content (timely threads, product updates, takes on current events). Kahl and McKenzie are right that evergreen content compounds; they are describing essays that answer a question a buyer will have in 2027 the same way they have it today. The operator error is treating these as the same thing.

The implication for an AI-services consultant in 2026: bias your content portfolio toward evergreen mechanism-explanation (Format C teardowns, Slot 3 working artifacts, Slot 4 live-debate positions with durable relevance) and away from the feed-participation cycle (hot takes on yesterday's model release, benchmark-number reactions, Twitter-drama threads). The evergreen side compounds; the feed side is Levels' faucet and you should know you're running it if you choose to.

## Layer 4 — The creator-consultant cognitive-dissonance trap

Here is the failure mode the brief names, and it is the one that catches the best operators. At some revenue mix, the content stops serving the service and starts replacing it. The transition is invisible until two or three quarters after it's happened.

The pattern, in roughly the order it unfolds:

1. Operator launches a consulting practice. Writes one post that goes unexpectedly well. Pipeline increases.
2. Operator writes more. Gets invited to speak, guest-post, appear on podcasts. Content becomes 20% of time, pipeline increases further.
3. Operator notices that a cohort-based course selling their method would be easier, more scalable, and has better margins than one-on-one consulting. Launches it. Course is successful.
4. Operator is now 50% content, 30% course, 20% consulting. The consulting is the unit that *produces* the case studies that sell the course that produces the content that attracts the next cohort.
5. Operator stops taking new consulting clients because they are "full" or because the margin math favors course over retainer. The feedback loop that made the content valuable (real client work surfacing real problems) is now running on old engagements.
6. Eighteen to thirty-six months later, the content starts to feel recycled, the courses feel dated, and the operator returns to consulting to "re-ground" — at which point they discover the market has moved, their reference-client network has cooled, and the frontier client problems no longer match their cached frameworks.

This is the Indie Hackers "building in public may be losing its shine" thread in concentrated form. The December 2024 community post and its follow-on "founders are losing faith in building in public" cited one quote that captures the dynamic: *"When everyone builds in public, no one builds in public."*[^9] The December 2024 Hacker News thread on building-in-public operators being "trapped" in audience maintenance rather than product building pushes the same point from the indie-dev side: the content flywheel becomes the job, and the underlying product or service, which was the original business, atrophies.[^10]

Two named specific cases worth studying as instruction:

- **Sahil Lavingia / Gumroad.** Lavingia publicly disclosed Gumroad's 2023 financials — $20.7M revenue, $8.9M net profit, zero full-time employees, in a January 2024 X post that remains a canonical building-in-public receipt.[^5] Lavingia's essay *"Reflecting on My Failure to Build a Billion-Dollar Company"* is the disconfirming companion piece: he names the period when audience-maintenance and content-output pulled him away from product decisions that compounded, and identifies it in his own retrospective as a drift rather than a strategy.[^15] The receipts-disclosure thread and the reflection essay together are the mature pattern — publish the numbers *and* the mechanism, including the mechanism of your own failure modes.
- **Daniel Vassallo / Small Bets.** Vassallo is perhaps the most visible case of the creator-consultant collapse resolved *into* content as the product. Small Bets, the community he built on the thesis of building a portfolio of small revenue streams, was acquired by Gumroad in April 2025 for $3.6M, split evenly between cash and stock options vesting over five years, with Vassallo continuing to operate it for five years.[^11] The content *became* the product; the content *sold* the content. This is a coherent outcome — but notice that Vassallo is no longer a consultant. He is the operator of a community-and-course business. If you are reading this lesson to build an AI-services consulting practice, Vassallo's path is a cautionary tale, not a template; it is what happens when the content-as-product loop closes successfully and the original service is no longer there when you want it back.

The diagnostic: compute your last-twelve-months revenue mix across (a) new consulting engagements, (b) existing retainer or course revenue, (c) content-derived revenue (sponsorships, paid newsletter, merchandise, ad revenue). If (c) + (b from courses) is above ~60% and (a) is below ~20%, you are no longer a consultant who publishes; you are a creator who occasionally consults. The 60% / 20% thresholds are operator-heuristic, not an empirically-established benchmark — they are the ratios I have seen separate the two business shapes in operator revenue disclosures (Welsh, Vassallo, Clouse, Abdaal on one side; Husain, McKenzie, Kahl-pre-Podscan on the other), not numbers from a published dataset. Treat them as diagnostic thresholds to think with, not cut-offs to trust to the decimal. That is a legitimate business model. It is not the business model this week is teaching. Know which one you are running.

## Operator war stories — named revenue disclosures

Three operator stories with named numbers, dates, and mechanisms. These are the disclosures that function as receipts for the people who published them — and as instruction for you.

**Story 1 — Jon Yongfook / Bannerbear: $50K to $991.4K in public.** Yongfook ran Bannerbear as an "open startup" from early days, publishing all revenue numbers on a public dashboard. The trajectory is legible: $10K MRR in January 2021, ~$52K MRR by late 2023, and annual revenue of $991.4K in 2024 (up from $630K in 2023), crossing $1M ARR on a Rails 6 + jQuery stack — technical simplicity he has repeatedly cited as evidence that "technical sophistication matters less than solving real problems effectively."[^6] Yongfook's operator rule, stated on the Indie Hackers podcast and his own blog: "If you want to go from 0 to $10K MRR you should divide your time 50:50 between coding and marketing." The content output (blogs, tutorials, docs) was *the marketing* — evergreen, SEO-weighted, product-adjacent. This is Position-C evergreen-asset content, executed for five years with a specific cadence that shipped hundreds of thousands in ARR.

**Story 2 — Pieter Levels / PhotoAI: $100K MRR without a content library.** Levels launched PhotoAI in February 2023. The product reached $100K/month in revenue by September 2024, approximately 18 months post-launch, and was at $132-138K MRR by November 2025.[^1] Levels publishes numbers, but crucially, his content is *not* evergreen — it is timely, conversational, opinion-led X/Twitter posting and occasional Hacker News comments (the November 2024 HN thread on Interior AI margins is representative: "Interior AI now has >99% profit margins — GPU bill is minimal").[^2] The shelf is thin by the standards of Layer 1 — no flagship case study in Husain's sense, no long-form teardowns, no multi-year essay archive. Levels' distribution is his X following (~600K) and the brand-as-default position he earned early in the indie-hacker category. Instructive: Position B's faucet thesis is empirically supported by Levels' own receipts. The moat is the product economics (near-zero marginal cost, API-wrapping with AI-generated images that cost cents to produce and sell for dollars); the content is the faucet; the business still works at $1.6M+ ARR.

**Story 3 — Arvid Kahl / Podscan: compounding without a revenue-disclosure post.** Kahl's 2024 year-in-review is revealing for what it does *not* disclose. Unlike the earlier era of Bootstrapped Founder newsletters — where he openly shared FeedbackPanda revenue and later reflected on selling it in 2019 — the 2024 Podscan review mentions funding from the Calm Company Fund without disclosing the amount, no subscriber counts, no MRR, no ARR. The Calm Company Fund mention is itself the under-read signal: once an outside investor is on the cap table, the disclosure calculus changes from "what serves the author's personal brand" to "what serves the portfolio company's competitive position," and institutional capital has its own opinions about revenue transparency when copycats can spin up a Podscan-adjacent product in a weekend. One quantifiable operational claim: "over 50,000 podcast episodes processed daily."[^3] The notable absence of financial transparency, from the operator most associated with in-public transparency, is itself the data point. Kahl's shift from revenue-disclosure to operational-metric disclosure tracks the December 2024 Indie Hackers observation that "founders who once championed radical transparency now going dark, deleting MRR updates, scrubbing product URLs from bios, and retreating into stealth mode to protect their business from copycats."[^9] The content engine still runs; the receipts have become selective. This is the mature operator pattern — disclosure calibrated to what serves the business, not a reflex of transparency for its own sake.

The through-line across all three: the *form* of the receipts is an instrument, not a value. Yongfook discloses everything because his buyers (other SaaS operators, developers evaluating an image-automation API) read numbers as the decisive signal. Levels discloses selectively because his buyers (consumers buying a $20 photo subscription) read product quality as the decisive signal. Kahl discloses selectively-differently because his audience has changed from founders-who-want-to-copy-him to founders-who-want-to-buy-Podscan. Match your disclosure shape to the buyer you want.

## Runnable experiment — draft your case study in three formats, pick the buyer

This is the working session. You will produce a real case study, in three forms, and identify the buyer you want before you let the format choose itself.

**Input you need:** one AI engagement you have done — client, internal, or meaningful side project — where you can describe the before state, the intervention, and the after state with specific numbers. If you cannot produce specific numbers, this is the first finding: your engagement was not instrumented, and no amount of case-study craft will fix that. Go back and add instrumentation to the next engagement.

**Phase 1 — three-format draft.** Open Claude Code. Give it the following instruction, with your engagement filled in:

> *"I want to publish a case study on a project I did [describe the project in 3–5 sentences: client/context, problem, what you built, outcome, honest caveat]. Draft it in three versions, each ~400 words:*
>
> *(a) Narrative version — story arc, minimal numbers, emphasis on diagnosis and decision. Mid-market CMO or Head-of-Ops buyer.*
>
> *(b) Receipts-heavy version — numbers first, minimal story, every claim backed by a metric. CFO/procurement buyer.*
>
> *(c) Teardown version — before/after artifact, mechanism explanation, rubric or prompt shown. VP of Engineering / Head of AI buyer.*
>
> *For each, include one honest line about what didn't work or what you'd do differently. Format as three Markdown sections."*

Read all three. The one you find easiest to write is almost certainly not the one that produces the buyer you want.

**Phase 2 — adversarial review.** Ask Claude to play a skeptical peer:

> *"Now play a skeptical senior AI consultant reading each version. For each version, steelman why the other two versions are more useful for their stated buyer. Be specific about what's weak in this version — vague numbers, missing mechanism, story that doesn't travel past my industry, etc."*

Write down the critique for each version. This is your revision guide.

**Phase 3 — identify the buyer, then the format.** Before revising, answer on paper — not in Claude — the following: which buyer type would most move your business in the next 90 days? If it's a CFO at a mid-market services firm, revise Version B. If it's a VP of Engineering at a Series-B SaaS, revise Version C. If it's a Head of Ops at a traditional industry, revise Version A. Do not revise all three; you do not have the time and they will each attract a different pipeline.

**Phase 4 — publish gate.** Before publishing, run a five-item gate: (i) are the numbers defensible under NDA review, (ii) is the honest-caveat line genuine or performatively humble, (iii) does the mechanism section describe the *why* not just the *what*, (iv) would a peer I respect read this and learn something, (v) can the buyer I wrote it for find it (right platform, right keywords, right distribution)? If any answer is no, hold it. A published case study that doesn't hit the intended buyer is noise in your shelf.

## Problem set

Five problems. Each has either a correct answer or a defensible written position. Do not skip; the writing is the learning.

**Problem 1 — publish (or draft-to-publish-ready) a real case study.** Using one real engagement and the three-format process above, produce one complete, publish-ready case study. Include specific numbers (or the explicit reason you cannot), a named honest caveat, and the buyer you wrote it for. Deliverable: a Markdown file, dated, ≤800 words. Pass/fail criterion: a peer in your field, reading it cold, can answer "who is this for and what would it get me to do next" without re-reading.

**Problem 2 — take a position on the imitators thesis.** The Indie Hackers December 2024 community thread and the Hacker News "trapped" thread both argue that building in public attracts imitators as much or more than customers.[^9][^10] Write a 300-500 word position: does this critique apply to AI-services consultants specifically, or is it an indie-SaaS-specific problem that doesn't transfer? Cite at least three data points (named operators, named outcomes, dates). Defensible-position criterion: your position could be stated on a panel with an operator from the other side without you reaching for generalities.

**Problem 3 — design your receipts shelf.** Using the seven-slot framework in Layer 1, produce a one-page plan: what artifact sits in each slot *today*, what would sit in each slot twelve months from today, and what is the single next artifact you will publish. Do this on paper first, then transcribe. For slots where you have nothing today, say so explicitly — do not list intentions as assets. Pass/fail: a skeptical peer reviewing the shelf can tell you which slot is load-bearing for your current positioning and which is dead weight.

**Problem 4 — reverse-engineer a named operator's content-to-revenue link.** Pick one operator from this lesson — Hamel Husain, Jon Yongfook, Pieter Levels, Arvid Kahl, Patrick McKenzie, or Sahil Lavingia. Read or re-read twelve months of their public output. Write a 400-600-word analysis: does their content explain their revenue? Specifically, where is the causal arrow — does the content produce pipeline, does the product produce content, or are they uncorrelated? Cite specific posts, specific dates, specific numbers. Pass/fail: a reader who has not done the exercise could read your analysis and stake a position of their own.

**Problem 5 — name the content you avoid publishing.** Identify one type of content you currently avoid producing because of perceived risk — salary transparency, a failure post-mortem, a disagreement with a prominent operator, a position on a live debate where most of your peers are hedged. Write one paragraph naming it. Then either (a) commit in writing to publishing it within 30 days, with the date and platform stated, or (b) explicitly decline with a specific reason (legal, contractual, reputational) and state what alternative artifact fills the same proof slot. No generic "I'll work on this." Either a committed publish date or an explicit decline with the reason named. Pass/fail: the statement is specific enough that a peer could hold you accountable.

## Common failure modes at scale

Patterns that recur across operators running this playbook for 18+ months. Each has a specific operator footprint.

- **Vanity receipts: the numbers are real, the context is absent.** Posting "hit $50K MRR" without the mechanism, the margin, the churn, the retention window, or the refund rate. A buyer reads it as a signal of momentum with no diagnostic value. Yongfook's Bannerbear Open Startup page is the counter-example: every metric is contextualized with the operating period and the product state.[^6]
- **Receipts inflation: reporting ARR when you mean contracted, or MRR when you mean booked.** The first time a buyer does a second-order reference check and discovers the number meant something looser than they assumed, the entire shelf is devalued. Err on the side of disclosing the definition alongside the number.
- **Case-study monoculture: twelve teardowns, zero financial-buyer versions.** The operator with an impressive technical shelf who cannot sell to the CFO-led buyer. This usually manifests as "we have great pipeline but all the deals stall at procurement." Diagnostic: count your last ten published artifacts; if more than seven are Format C, you have a buyer problem, not a content problem.
- **The silent pivot to creator business.** Revenue mix drifts to course + sponsorship + paid newsletter, the operator keeps calling themselves a consultant, and the positioning frays. This is the Vassallo path run accidentally rather than intentionally.[^11] Run the quarterly revenue-mix diagnostic named in Layer 4.
- **Copycat paralysis.** Operator stops publishing numbers because competitors used the disclosures to target their customers or replicate their product. This is the Indie Hackers 2024 "going dark" pattern.[^9] It is a real cost and a legitimate reason to selective-disclose — but note that the operators who went fully dark often also stopped getting *new* pipeline, because the shelf went stale. The mature pattern is Kahl's selective disclosure — keep operational metrics, retire the revenue-receipt thread, maintain the case-study output.[^3]
- **Publishing to an empty room and calling it a moat.** The writer who produces one evergreen essay a month for three years to a mailing list of 400 cold readers. The content is compounding; the distribution is not. Fix the distribution (Slot 7) before adding more artifacts (Slots 1–5).

## Open questions — what's not settled

Three live debates worth watching through the next four quarters.

**(1) AI-generated content collapses the evergreen-asset thesis — or doesn't.** If a competitor can generate a credible-looking Format-B case study or Format-C teardown with GPT-5 or Claude 4-level models in a weekend, the compounding moat around human-written evergreen content thins. The counter: AI-generated content still fails the "mechanism explanation" test that real clients can audit. The resolution is probably category-specific; technical teardowns may survive longer than narrative case studies. Watch the 2026 Buffer State of Creator Economy and MicroConf State of Indie SaaS reports for the signal.[^14][^16]

**(2) The going-dark trend is either the right move or premature.** The 2024 Indie Hackers thread of operators deleting MRR updates may be a rational response to copycat risk, or it may be the top of an attention cycle that will produce regret in 2027 when those operators find they've lost distribution. The test: track the three operators who most visibly went dark in 2024 and measure their pipeline and revenue in 2026. If they have held or grown, selective disclosure is the new equilibrium; if they have softened, the transparency regime still has positive ROI and the going-dark was a panic.

**(3) Building-in-public for AI consultants specifically is under-instrumented.** Most of the public data — Indie Hackers, MicroConf, Open Startup — is SaaS-product data, not services-consulting data. The mechanics of receipts-based sales for $50K–$500K AI-services engagements are probably different from $20/month product subscriptions, but the comparable-scale services literature is thin. If you publish a serious account of your own services revenue with mechanism intact, you are contributing to a dataset that doesn't yet exist. That's a reason to publish, not a reason to wait for someone else to.

## Reviewer lens — named critics with specific disagreements

Five named operators with specific pushbacks on specific lines in this lesson.

- **Pieter Levels (levelsio on X; 2024 HN thread on Interior AI margins).**[^2] Would push back on Layer 3's "Position C is correct" resolution. His argument, consistent across years of X threads: for *product* businesses, content is never a moat and rarely even an asset worth the opportunity cost — the moat is product economics (margin per unit, distribution default, brand-as-category). He would argue that the lesson's services-consultant framing smuggles in an assumption — that the reader's business is a consulting practice — that collapses if the reader's actual business is building AI products. For a product operator, Slot 7 (distribution) and product quality matter; Slots 1–6 are discretionary marketing at best. Counter-weight: this lesson is explicitly for AI *services* consultants; Levels' critique applies to the brief's subject, not the reader this brief is for.
- **Arvid Kahl (thebootstrappedfounder.com; 2024 year-in-review).**[^3] Would push back on Layer 4's diagnostic ratio (revenue mix, content-derived >60% = creator, not consultant). His position, implied in the Podscan operator pattern, is that the consultant-to-operator transition is itself a legitimate strategic path and the diagnostic ratio reads more like a moral injunction than an economic analysis. Specifically, the line "That is a legitimate business model. It is not the business model this week is teaching" may be *too* sharp — some readers should take the Kahl/Vassallo path deliberately, and the lesson could point them to it rather than frame it as a trap. Counter-weight: the brief explicitly names this as a trap, and the framing is for readers who are trying to build services practices, not readers who have already decided to pivot.
- **Hamel Husain (hamel.dev; April 2025 *Field Guide*).**[^7] Would push back on Layer 2's framing of Format C ("teardown") as bleeding information competitors can copy. His *Field Guide* and eval-course materials explicitly publish the method, and Husain has stated (on Lenny's Podcast, April 2025) that "giving away the method" has been the opposite of commoditizing for him — the more explicit the method, the harder it is for non-practitioners to execute, and the more valuable the person who wrote it becomes as the hired expert.[^8] The lesson's Layer-2 weakness-of-Format-C description may overweight a concern that Husain's revenue trajectory disconfirms. Counter-weight: Husain's position assumes the method is genuinely hard to execute even when explicit, which is a claim about the method's complexity, not a general rule.
- **Patrick McKenzie (kalzumeus.com greatest hits; bitsaboutmoney.com).**[^12][^13] Would push back on Layer 3's labeling of his position as "narrower" than Kahl's. His actual position, read carefully in the kalzumeus archive, is that evergreen content is an asset with a specific production function (perpetually-recurring question × well-written answer × SEO structure × time) and that the "compounding" language obscures the production work required. A reader who hears "compounding asset" and imagines passive yield will not do the production work and will get a faucet, not an asset. McKenzie would want the Layer 3 resolution to more visibly distinguish *compounding because you keep producing* from *compounding because of a mystical time-based effect*. Counter-weight: Layer 3 does note this, but could state it more clearly in the synthesis.
- **Sahil Lavingia (shl on X; January 2024 Gumroad financials disclosure; *Reflecting on My Failure to Build a Billion-Dollar Company*).**[^5][^15] Would push back on Story 1's implicit endorsement of the Gumroad-financials disclosure pattern as mature. His own retrospective essay explicitly identifies the period of heavy public-disclosure-plus-content-output as *drift*, not strategy — time he could have spent on product decisions that compounded more than the content did. The lesson quotes the receipt but does not fully credit the disconfirming reflection: the operator who published the canonical disclosure has, in his own words, indicated that disclosure did not cause the compounding he wanted. Counter-weight: the reflection is cited in Layer 4, but the contrast could be sharper in Story 1.
