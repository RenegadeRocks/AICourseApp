# Niche positioning and category creation — Dunford's structure, Raskin's narrative, Lochhead's escape

## Why this matters

You spent Friday proving a niche. Today you learn what to do with it on the page, in the inbox, and on the call — because a validated niche that cannot be stated in eighty legible words is a niche that your buyer will round off to the nearest generic label ("AI consultant," "automation person," "prompt engineer") and price accordingly. Three traditions argue over how to do this. April Dunford says positioning is a five-component structural problem you can solve on a whiteboard in an afternoon. Andy Raskin says the load-bearing move is a strategic narrative — a story about the world your buyer is living in — and that positioning without narrative sells features. Christopher Lochhead and the *Play Bigger* school say both of them are playing a smaller game than they need to: the only position that compounds is the one where you create the category and become its king. Each claims the others are incomplete. Each is partially right.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to (1) write a Dunford-structured positioning statement in under eighty words and defend each component against an operator who disagrees, (2) translate that positioning into a Raskin-shaped strategic narrative that makes your buyer's world change rather than your product's features stack, (3) apply the Lochhead category-creation test honestly — including the part where it usually says *don't* — and know when a niche-within-category, an adjacency crossover, or a genuine category-creation play is the right bet for your offer, (4) teardown live 2024–2026 category plays (AI SDR, AI code editor, vibe coding, AI legal, AI customer support) and identify which won, which fragmented, which collapsed under fraud allegations, and why, and (5) hold a defensible position on whether category creation is a brilliant strategic move, a vanity project, or a bet only the well-capitalized can make — and which of those you are. This lesson closes Week 2 by converting Friday's niche-validation math into the pitch, website headline, outbound opener, and investor-deck slide that a buyer and a peer can both parse in ten seconds.

## Prerequisites

- You have completed Friday's niche-validation exercise or equivalent: a 100-account bottom-up, a draft unit-economics model, and at least one falsifiable niche hypothesis. Positioning without a validated niche is decoration.
- You have read, or are willing to reference while we work, April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* (2019) OR her Quickstart Guide.[^1] The lesson does not require the book — we operationalize what we use — but the worked examples in Chapter 6 pay for themselves.

## Layer 1 — Dunford's five components, and the beginner mistake on each

There are at least three ways to get positioning wrong. You can be in the wrong category. You can have the right category but the wrong alternatives. You can have the right alternatives but fail to convert unique attributes into buyer-legible value. Dunford's structural contribution — refined across *Obviously Awesome* (2019) and *Sales Pitch* (2023) — is a five-component schema that makes each of these failure modes visible before it ships.[^1][^2] The components, in her recommended order of operations:

**1. Competitive alternatives.** What does the buyer do if your offer does not exist? Dunford's test: if you can't name the real alternatives, your whole positioning collapses, because value is only legible against alternatives.[^1] For AI-services operators, the beginner mistake is listing other AI consultants. The real alternatives are almost always (a) the in-house team doing the work poorly, (b) a consulting firm charging 3× for slower delivery, (c) a SaaS product solving 40% of the problem, and (d) doing nothing — the buyer tolerating the pain because no option feels safe. The correct answer is usually three of these simultaneously, not one.

**2. Unique attributes.** What do you actually have that alternatives don't? Dunford is strict here: attributes must be *features or capabilities* that are verifiable, not personality ("we care more") or posture ("we're faster"). For an AI-services operator, attributes might be *a shipped eval harness for your vertical*, *a pre-built RAG corpus on the client's document class*, *a pricing model indexed to outcomes rather than tokens*, *a named operator reputation in the specific subcategory*. The beginner mistake is listing attributes that every competitor also has ("we use Claude Sonnet 4.6" is not an attribute; it's table stakes).

**3. Value.** What do the attributes enable for the buyer, stated as a business outcome? Dunford's rule: value is what attributes *deliver*, not what attributes *are*.[^1] The attribute "pre-built eval harness for contract review" produces the value "we can certify a 90% pass rate before you sign a pilot, so your legal team doesn't have to run their own parallel review for three months." The beginner mistake is conflating the two — listing the harness as if its existence *is* the value. It isn't; the value is the skipped parallel review.

**4. Best-fit customer.** Who cares disproportionately about this value? Dunford's test: if the value is universally valuable, you haven't narrowed enough. You should be able to name the buyer segment tightly enough that it excludes more prospects than it includes.[^1] For AI services, this is *vertical × capability × buyer-seniority* — the three-axis model from Thursday — and the failure mode is hedging on all three axes at once ("we help B2B companies with AI").

**5. Market category.** What context makes the value obvious? Dunford's definition: the category is not a taxonomy you're forced into; it's a frame you select because it makes the buyer instantly legible to themselves about why they need you.[^1] The beginner mistake is defaulting to whatever category label the market currently uses. If you sell contract-review automation to mid-market legal ops, "AI consulting" is the wrong category; "legal-ops automation" is the right one. The category determines the reference set the buyer unconsciously compares you to — and the reference set is where pricing power lives.

The operational trap across all five: treating the output as a static statement rather than a live hypothesis. Dunford's published worked examples — the mobile-database company that repositioned from "Microsoft Access killer" to "embeddable database for mobile devices" and went from near-failure to $1.3B revenue, the investment-banking CRM that went from $2M to $80M in 18 months by repositioning against Siebel specifically rather than against generic CRM — both reposition by changing the *category* component first, which then cascades through alternatives, attributes, and value.[^1] The five components are interdependent; moving one correctly often means moving all five.

## Layer 2 — Raskin's strategic narrative, and why Dunford's statement isn't enough

Andy Raskin has worked with Uber, Salesforce, Square, Gong, Dropbox, and IBM on what he calls *strategic narrative* — a story the whole leadership team tells, consistently, across sales, marketing, product, fundraising, and recruiting.[^3] His central critique of positioning work, including Dunford's, is not that positioning is wrong. It's that *positioning is insufficient to move a buyer*. A Dunford statement tells your sales team what to anchor on; a strategic narrative tells the *buyer* why their world just changed.

Raskin's five-part structure:[^4]

1. **Name the new game (and the old one).** There is a shift happening. The rules of success are changing.
2. **Show there are life-and-death stakes.** Winners and losers. Not incremental improvement — category-level consequence.
3. **Define the object of the new game.** What, specifically, is the buyer trying to win at now that they weren't before?
4. **Present the obstacles to winning.** Why most players will fail at the new game even though they know about it.
5. **Show how you help them overcome the obstacles.** Your offer as the instrument, not the hero.

Raskin's signature worked example: Drift, founded 2014 by David Cancel and Elias Torres, did not market itself as "chat software for websites" (which would have put it against Intercom and Olark in a saturated category).[^5] It named a new game — *conversational marketing* — with explicit stakes: buyers now expect immediate, two-way interaction, and companies still running the old game (forms, delayed email follow-up, inside-sales cadences) will systematically lose to the companies running the new one. The obstacle to winning the new game is not "lacking chat software"; it is "still organized around lead forms and delayed nurture." Drift is the instrument. Drift published a book, ran a conference called *HYPERGROWTH*, and treated category evangelism as a marketing primitive — on-ramps that HubSpot had used a decade earlier around *inbound marketing*.[^5][^6] Drift sold to Salesloft in 2024; the category they named outlasted the acquisition.[^5]

Raskin's distinction between "old game / new game" and "old way / new way" is load-bearing. *Old way / new way* produces product-led stories: "the old way is forms, the new way is chat." *Old game / new game* produces buyer-mindset stories: "the old game was capturing leads efficiently; the new game is building real-time relationships with buyers who now expect to be talked with, not at."[^4] The first sells a feature; the second changes how a VP of Marketing thinks about their job. Raskin's argument is that only the second shifts buyer behavior at the scale that unlocks premium pricing.

**Where Raskin and Dunford visibly disagree.** Dunford is explicit that strategic narrative, as Raskin practices it, is downstream of positioning — you build a narrative *after* you've nailed the five components, and the narrative is a delivery vehicle, not the foundation.[^1] Raskin's implicit counter, visible in his Medium archive and Lenny's Podcast appearance, is that positioning produced in isolation from narrative tends to become sales collateral the buyer never reads; narrative is where the buyer's internal story gets reshaped, and without that reshape, no statement converts.[^3][^4] **My operating position — stated as a position, with named operators on each side:** *run both, in the order that matches your evidence base.* Drift (Cancel/Torres) built narrative-first — "conversational marketing" as the new-game/old-game frame was in place before the five-component Dunford statement was ever cleaned up, and the narrative pulled product, sales, and category into alignment afterward.[^5] Dunford's own consulting portfolio (the mobile-database case, the investment-banking CRM case) ran positioning-first — the five components were rewritten in an afternoon, the repositioning cascaded, and narrative came after the structure was sound.[^1] The deciding variable is how much evidence you have about your buyer's current mental model: if you already know the old-game/new-game shift your buyer is living through (Drift's case, where Cancel/Torres had spent years inside buyer calls), run narrative-first and let the five components fall out. If you don't yet know it and the risk is projecting your own story onto the buyer, run Dunford-first to force honesty about alternatives and value, then write the Raskin narrative once the structure is anchored. If you can only do one, do both anyway — the order matters less than the fact that an operator without either lives in feature-pitch territory.

**Applied to an AI-services operator.** Suppose you are an independent consultant selling AI-augmented financial-close acceleration to mid-market CFOs. The Dunford structure locates you: category is "close-automation" not "AI consulting"; alternatives are the in-house FP&A team working late, a Big Four engagement at 5× your fee, and tools like Blackline configured with native AI modules; unique attribute is an eval harness that benchmarks reconciliation accuracy against the client's own last-four-quarter close; value is cutting a ten-day close to five while producing an auditable accuracy log; best-fit buyer is VP Finance at a PE-owned mid-market company under close-timeline pressure from the sponsor. That's legible. The Raskin narrative layered on top goes: *the old game was closing the books accurately within ten days; the new game is closing within five with a defensible AI-assisted audit trail, because PE sponsors are now benchmarking portfolio companies against their AI-native peers. Companies still running the old game will be marked down at sale. The obstacle is that reconciliation accuracy under AI assistance is unaudited; you need evals that survive diligence. I build those evals.* Notice the narrative doesn't mention your company until the last sentence. That's the shape.

## Layer 3 — Lochhead's escape: when category creation is the right move, and when it destroys you

*Play Bigger*, co-authored by Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, and Kevin Maney (2016), makes a sharper claim than either Dunford or Raskin. Their research, published as the Time-to-Market-Cap (TTMC) report, argues that *category kings* — the companies that define and dominate a new category — capture roughly 76% of the total market capitalization of their category.[^7][^8] Not 76% of the leader's segment. Seventy-six percent of the *entire category*. The remaining 24% is split across all other players combined. If the number holds, the second-place finisher in a category gets a fraction of a fraction; the third-place finisher is noise.

The Lochhead operational conclusion: *niche positioning inside a category someone else created is a structurally losing game*, because the category king has already captured the bulk of the economics. Repositioning within their category — being the "better" or "cheaper" or "niche-specialized" version of them — is competing over the residual. The only move that changes the economics is to *create a different category* in which you are the king, not the challenger. *Play Bigger* calls this "category design": naming the problem differently, defining the buyer mission in new terms, and executing a "lightning strike" — a concentrated, expensive marketing move that establishes the category in the buyer's mind before competitors can contest the definition.[^7][^8]

The examples *Play Bigger* runs on are Salesforce ("The End of Software," 1999), Uber, Amazon, Netflix, Slack, HubSpot ("inbound marketing," 2006), and Drift ("conversational marketing," 2016).[^6][^8][^9] Salesforce in particular — Marc Benioff hired actors to hold anti-software signs outside Siebel's user conference, themed its launch at the Regency Theater around "The End of Software," and wrote a category-creation campaign that ran for roughly a decade — is the canonical case.[^9] The outcome validates the theory: Salesforce went from contested startup to category king to market cap above $300B by 2025. Siebel, which had been winning the old category, was absorbed by Oracle.

**Where Lochhead and Dunford visibly disagree.** Dunford's 2024 interview on the *SaaS Half Full* podcast, titled "It's a Crapshoot: Category Creation & Positioning," states her counter-position in unusually direct terms. She argues that fewer than 35 companies successfully created and dominated a category between 2000 and 2015 — out of tens of thousands of venture-backed attempts — and that the mathematical base rate makes category creation an irresponsible strategy for most founders.[^10] *Provenance note:* the "<35 in 15 years" figure is Dunford's specific claim from that 2024 interview, anchored on her own reading of the TTMC Category Kings dataset[^7] — it is not an independently replicated academic finding. The directionally stronger evidence is the TTMC research itself (which identifies roughly 35 Category Kings in the same period); Dunford's contribution is the *interpretive move* of reading that as a base-rate ceiling for intentional category creation. Operators should treat the number as Dunford-inflected, not as a peer-reviewed constant — the direction of the argument (category creation is rare) is robust, the precise denominator is her framing. Her stronger claim in the same interview: *categories emerge; companies don't create them.* The companies Lochhead celebrates as category creators are, on her reading, companies that were smart enough to recognize an emerging category and claim the frame; the category would have emerged without them.[^10] Her prescription: niche inside an existing category, be the best-fit solution for a defined segment, and treat category creation as a lottery ticket rather than a plan.

Lochhead's counter, articulated repeatedly on his *Christopher Lochhead Follow Your Different* podcast and in *Category Pirates* writing, is that Dunford's framework is correct *inside* a category but conservative *about* categories, and that the 76% number dominates any base-rate argument: even if category creation succeeds 1% of the time, the payoff makes it the correct strategy for operators who can afford the bet, because the expected value dominates the alternatives.[^7][^8][^11]

**The honest synthesis most working operators settle on:** category creation is correct strategy for a small number of companies and wrong strategy for most. The filter is not ambition; it is capitalization and distribution. A venture-backed company with eighteen months of runway, a marketing budget in the mid-seven-figures, and a founder willing to spend 40% of their time evangelizing a new vocabulary can credibly run a category-creation play. A solo AI consultant with a four-figure monthly marketing budget cannot — for them, the correct Lochhead-adjacent move is *sub-category creation* inside a larger category whose king is already established. "AI contract-review specialist for mid-market legal ops" is a defensible sub-category inside "legal AI"; trying to replace "legal AI" with a new category name is, for a one-person firm, a vanity project.

## Layer 4 — Named category teardowns, 2024–2026 — successes, partial wins, and collapses

Categories in the AI-services market are being named, contested, and discarded on roughly eighteen-month cycles in 2024–2026. Running teardowns on specific named plays is how you calibrate which moves generalize and which were surface-level.

**Teardown 1 — "Vibe coding" (2025): category naming wins even when the namer has no product.** Andrej Karpathy tweeted the phrase "vibe coding" on February 2, 2025, describing a workflow in which the developer "fully gives in to the vibes, embraces exponentials, and forgets that the code even exists," enabled by tools like Cursor Composer with Claude Sonnet and SuperWhisper for voice input.[^12] The term was listed by Merriam-Webster as "slang & trending" in March 2025 and named Collins English Dictionary Word of the Year 2025.[^12] Karpathy shipped no product, filed no trademark, and captured no direct economic value. But the term reframed the reference set: Cursor, Replit, GitHub Copilot, and Windsurf are now routinely referred to as "vibe coding tools" in tech press, which (a) frames them as a new-game category distinct from traditional IDEs and (b) raises the unit-value conversation above IDE pricing norms. The lesson: a category name with high memetic fit can do work *even when the namer has no product*, because it reframes how buyers think about the entire reference set, and whoever sits best inside the frame captures the pricing uplift. Cursor, which crossed $1B annualized ARR in November 2025 with Anysphere valuation implied above $9.9B as of June 2025, is the primary economic beneficiary.[^13]

**Teardown 2 — "AI SDR" (2024–2025): category created, then contaminated.** 11x.ai, founded by Hasan Sukkar in 2022, raised a Series B of $50M led by Andreessen Horowitz in late 2024, marketing "Alice" and "Julian" as autonomous AI sales development reps.[^14] The category label "AI SDR" got traction quickly — by mid-2025, multiple vendors (Artisan, Regie.ai, Clay, AiSDR) were competing under the same label. In March 2025, TechCrunch reported that 11x had claimed customers it did not actually have on its website (including ZoomInfo, which publicly stated it had not authorized logo use), had been reporting ARR without differentiating trial periods from long-term contracts, and had a 70–80% churn rate; former engineers described the product as barely working.[^14][^15] Andreessen Horowitz publicly denied legal action plans. The aftermath: "AI SDR" as a category label became associated with over-claiming, and the 2026 GTM conversation has shifted to "AI-augmented outbound operator" and "human-in-the-loop prospecting" framings — category reset in progress. The lesson: naming a category early is valuable, but the category name becomes the reputation of its loudest vendor; if the loudest vendor cannot deliver, the category itself is taxed with that failure. Clay, which positioned itself adjacent to the category ("data enrichment and automation") rather than squarely in it, raised a Series B at $1.25B valuation in the same period and avoided the contamination.[^14][^16]

**Teardown 3 — "Ambient computing" (Humane, 2024): category wish, without buyer mandate.** Humane raised $230M at an $850M valuation and shipped the AI Pin in April 2024 at $699 plus $24/month, positioning the device in a declared category of *ambient computing* — a post-smartphone mode in which AI is present without a screen.[^17] The category name was coherent, the TED-stage narrative was elegant, and the product had backing from ex-Apple executives. By October 2024, returns exceeded $1M; in February 2025, HP acquired Humane's assets for $116M, a roughly $115M write-down on raised capital.[^17] Applying the Lochhead test: Humane had the marketing-budget capitalization to name a category, but the buyer had no mandate. The old game — using a smartphone — was not failing in a way anyone urgently wanted to escape, and the new game — "wearing ambient AI" — had no life-and-death stakes. The category was a wish, not a shift. The lesson: category creation requires an underlying *game shift* the buyer is already feeling, even if unnamed. Without that shift, you can spend the capitalization and still fail.

**Teardown 4 — "Inbound marketing" (HubSpot, 2006): category creation done at book-and-conference scale.** Brian Halligan and Dharmesh Shah co-founded HubSpot in 2006 in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Halligan had coined "inbound marketing" in 2005 while working at a VC firm, after observing that outbound tactics (cold calling, list buying, mass emailing) were losing effectiveness against pull-based tactics (search, social, content).[^18] The founders started a blog *before* the product existed, published a book of the same title, ran the INBOUND conference starting in 2008, and treated category evangelism as the primary marketing function.[^18] HubSpot IPO'd in 2014 and by 2025 held a market cap above $25B. The lesson: category creation at the HubSpot or Drift or Salesforce scale is not a tweet or a blog post; it is a decade-long investment in books, conferences, evangelist employees, and a consistent old-game/new-game frame that hundreds of thousands of buyers encounter repeatedly. The math of 76% category-king capture applies at this scale, not at solo-consultant scale.

**Teardown 5 — "AI evals" (Hamel Husain / Parlance Labs, 2024–2026): sub-category creation as the operator-scale move.** Hamel Husain, independent ML engineer and educator, positioned his consulting firm Parlance Labs around one specific sub-category: *evaluation-driven development* for LLM products. His public writing ("Your AI Product Needs Evals"), his Maven course with Shreya Shankar ("AI Evals for Engineers & PMs," with over 3,500 students including alumni from OpenAI, Meta, and Google), and his podcast appearances all reinforce the same frame: evals are the unglamorous, load-bearing primitive that separates shipped AI products from demos, and he is the operator to hire when you need one.[^19] **Important caveat on the framing.** The "sub-category creation" label is *this lesson's retrospective analytical frame*, not a claim Husain himself has made. Reading across hamel.dev and his public talks, what is visible is an operator who set out to solve a specific technical problem (LLM eval infrastructure) for a specific buyer (teams shipping AI products that need to pass internal quality gates), and the sub-category label emerged as the market's retrospective packaging of the work. The operator takeaway is not "set out to create a sub-category"; it is "set out to solve a narrow technical problem exceptionally well, accept that if the work compounds the market will eventually hand you a sub-category label, and do not optimize for the label ahead of the work." Operators who invert this order — naming the sub-category first, then reverse-engineering the work to fit — tend to over-position and under-deliver, because the label is only durable if the work underneath it is. With that caveat held, the observable outcome remains: Husain did not try to create a new top-level category ("AI consulting" already exists); the market ended up treating him as the reference operator in a sub-category ("AI evals specialist") that is small enough to dominate at one-person scale, narrow enough to price premium, and broad enough to scale into a firm (Parlance Labs' reference pricing — the canonical Week 2 figure established in Monday's footnote [^1] as approximately $285,500 per engagement by April 2026 — is the tangible output of this positioning). This is the realistic category-adjacent shape for operators who are not venture-backed: the Dunford "market category" you become legible inside can fit within someone else's larger category, and the operator-scale version of category work is closer to *earning* a sub-category label through concentrated technical output than to *declaring* one through marketing.

**Teardown 6 — "AI legal" (Harvey vs. legacy legaltech, 2024–2026): category the incumbent doesn't want to be in.** Harvey reached enterprise adoption at Reed Smith, Nixon Peabody, and other Am Law 100 firms in 2024–2025. Its positioning is as "the AI platform for legal professional services." The interesting move is not what Harvey claims but what it avoids claiming: it does not position itself against Westlaw or LexisNexis (legacy legaltech) because those incumbents' business model is priced per user-seat and Harvey's value unlocks only by disrupting that pricing model.[^20] Instead, Harvey positions against the *billable-hour model itself* — an implicit Raskin-shaped narrative in which the old game is pyramid-structured hourly labor and the new game is outcome-priced legal work. The category label "AI legal" is being contested by multiple players (Harvey, Spellbook, Ironclad AI, Robin AI), and the winning position is not yet settled. The lesson: when the category is contested, Dunford's "best-fit customer" component becomes the tiebreaker — the player who most sharply defines *who* the offer is for (AmLaw 100 vs. mid-market vs. in-house legal ops) captures the segment and defers the category-king question.

## Layer 5 — Repositioning moves: when the category you're in is actively harming you

Most AI-services operators do not start from zero. They started somewhere — "AI consultant," "automation specialist," "LLM engineer," "GenAI strategist" — and the position is now doing them active harm. The three most common harm signatures:

**Harm signature 1: the category is too broad, pulling in wrong-fit buyers.** If your inbound is 80% buyers who do not meet your ICP, the category label is over-indexing on generic demand. Dunford's fix: narrow the category ("AI consultant" → "RAG implementation specialist for regulated industries") and let the unfit inbound drop.[^1] The revenue pattern that confirms: higher reply rate on outbound to tight-ICP lists, lower reply rate on general inbound, rising close rates. The pattern that disconfirms: inbound collapses entirely, outbound doesn't compensate. The risk: narrowing too fast before the micro-TAM from Friday can sustain the funnel.

**Harm signature 2: the alternatives the buyer compares you to are setting your price.** If prospects compare you to "ChatGPT Teams subscription" ($30/user/month), your hourly or project rate looks absurd. If they compare you to "a Big Four engagement" ($500K+), your rate looks like a bargain. Dunford's fix: change the category component so the comparison set shifts.[^1] Harvey's implicit move from "legaltech software" to "strategic AI partner for AmLaw firms" is this exact play. Operator tactic: rewrite the headline on your site, the first line of your outbound, and the first slide of your proposal to reference the alternative set you *want* to be compared to, and notice whether prospects re-anchor.

**Harm signature 3: the category is contaminated by a bad-actor vendor.** If "AI SDR" now connotes the 11x TechCrunch story to your buyer, selling under that label means inheriting the reputational cost.[^14][^15] The fix is not to stay in the category and try to restore its reputation (hopeless at operator scale); it is to rename to an adjacent category where you are not taxed with someone else's failure. *Pavilion's 2025 piece "The 11X Fraud vs. The AI SDR Future"* argues that the category will continue but under new framings ("pipeline AI," "outbound agents," "AI prospecting operators"); operators who renamed out of "AI SDR" in Q1 2025 reported higher demo conversion than those who stayed.[^15]

**The repositioning protocol.** When you identify the harm signature, do not rewrite your positioning in isolation. Rewrite all five Dunford components simultaneously because they cascade. Change the category, and the alternatives change; change the alternatives, and the value proposition changes; change the value proposition, and the best-fit customer changes. Run the new five-component statement past five prospects in real conversations within two weeks. If three of five instantly understand and self-identify as in or out of fit, the repositioning holds. If two or fewer, you moved one component without cascading; iterate.

Dunford's own repositioning case studies — the mobile database moving from "Microsoft Access killer" to "embeddable mobile database" (from near-failure to $1.3B revenue), the investment-banking CRM moving from "generic CRM" to "relationship-modeling for investment banking" (from under $2M to $80M in 18 months) — both execute this full-cascade repositioning, with the category component changing first and everything else falling into place.[^1]

## Operator case studies / war stories

**Drift — category creation at scale, measurable outcome.** David Cancel and Elias Torres co-founded Drift in 2014. By 2016 they had named the category "conversational marketing," published a book of that title in 2019, run the HYPERGROWTH conference series starting 2017, and treated category-evangelism as a dedicated budget line. Drift reached over 50,000 customers, IPO'd speculation in 2020 before accepting private-equity acquisition, and was acquired by Salesloft in February 2024 for an undisclosed sum, with the combined entity continuing under the Salesloft name.[^5] The category label "conversational marketing" outlasted the company identity and is still in use by 2026 vendors. Operator lesson: a category created at scale is an asset that persists across the creator's exit.

**HubSpot — the ten-year category-creation investment.** HubSpot's inbound-marketing category was not an overnight naming. Halligan and Shah published a book, ran the INBOUND conference starting 2008 (reaching 25,000+ attendees annually by late 2010s), hired dozens of full-time evangelists, and ran a content engine that produced 100+ blog posts/month at peak.[^18] HubSpot IPO'd in 2014 and by 2025 held market cap above $25B. Operator lesson: HubSpot is the exception that proves Dunford's base-rate rule — the success is real and the investment required is roughly $50M+ in marketing and a decade of founder time.

**11x.ai — category creation with a fraudulent product.** 11x raised $24M Series A and $50M Series B in 2024, reached roughly $14M reported ARR, and was exposed by TechCrunch in March 2025 for claiming customers it did not have (ZoomInfo publicly denied authorization), for counting trial-period revenue as full-year ARR (only $3M of $14M survived trials per former employees), and for shipping a product with 70–80% churn and reports of core features not loading.[^14] Operator lesson: a category label can be built on sales narrative alone for roughly 12–18 months before product reality catches up; operators building category-adjacent positioning should price in the risk of the category's loudest vendor detonating.

## Runnable experiment — build, narrate, attack

This is a three-phase workflow you run end-to-end in a single session, using Claude Code and Claude.ai. It should take 90–120 minutes. You are the director; Claude is the instrument.

**Phase 1 — Build three Dunford-structured statements.** Open Claude Code. Paste the prompt below, filling in your own niche hypothesis from Friday.

> *You are April Dunford, operating in 2026. Based on my niche hypothesis — [PASTE YOUR HYPOTHESIS HERE, including vertical, capability, buyer seniority, and 1–2 sentence value claim] — draft three positioning statements, each under 80 words, each using the five-component structure (competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit customer, market category). Make them materially different:
> - Variant A: niche-within-an-existing-category (e.g., "AI consultant specializing in X").
> - Variant B: adjacency crossover (combining two categories, e.g., "finance-automation × AI-eval specialist").
> - Variant C: genuine category creation (a new category label you'd have to educate the market on).
> For each variant, list the five components explicitly, then give the 80-word statement.*

Read the three outputs. Do not iterate yet.

**Phase 2 — Translate each into a Raskin narrative and test with three buyer personas.** Paste the three statements back into Claude Code along with:

> *Now, for each of the three variants, write a Raskin-style strategic narrative in 150–200 words using his five-part structure: (1) name the new game and the old game, (2) show the life-and-death stakes, (3) define the object of the new game, (4) present obstacles to winning, (5) show how the offer helps overcome them. Do not mention my company until the last sentence. Then, for each variant, role-play three buyers in separate responses: a VP of Operations, a CMO, and a CFO at a mid-market company in my target vertical. For each role-play, have the buyer react to the narrative in their voice, naming what makes the offer legible, what confuses them, and whether the narrative raises or lowers the price ceiling they'd accept.*

You should now have nine buyer reactions (3 variants × 3 personas). This is the legibility test. A variant that confuses all three personas is dead on arrival. A variant that is legible to only one persona tells you your best-fit customer narrowed in an unexpected direction.

**Phase 3 — Steelman and counter-steelman the category-creation variant.** Take Variant C (the category-creation variant) and paste:

> *Steelman the strongest case FOR committing to Variant C for the next 12 months. Cite the Play Bigger 76% category-king thesis, name three historical analogs that succeeded (e.g., HubSpot's inbound marketing, Drift's conversational marketing, Salesforce's end-of-software), and estimate the minimum marketing investment required at operator scale. Then steelman the strongest case AGAINST, citing Dunford's 2024 "category creation is a crapshoot" position, the 11x AI SDR cautionary tale, and the base-rate argument that fewer than 35 companies created categories successfully 2000–2015. Name which of the three variants (A, B, or C) you would commit to for the next 12 months and defend the decision in 3 paragraphs with specific reasoning about my capitalization, distribution reach, and risk tolerance.*

Pick the variant you will stand behind for 12 months. Write it into a single-page document with the Dunford five components, the Raskin narrative, and your decision rationale. Date it. You will re-audit this document in six months.

## Problem set

**Problem 1 — Write and defend.** Write a Dunford-structured positioning statement for your current or planned offer in ≤80 words. List each of the five components on a separate line with a one-sentence defense of your choice. Pass criterion: an operator peer reading only your five components (not the 80-word statement) can predict the 80-word statement within 10% paraphrase. Fail criterion: they construct a materially different statement.

**Problem 2 — Take a position.** Take a written position, in 300–500 words, on whether category creation is strategically correct for your offer in 2026. Your position must cite at least two named operators on each side (e.g., Lochhead/Play Bigger, Drift/Cancel, HubSpot/Halligan on the YES side; Dunford, the 11x teardown, the base-rate argument on the NO side). Pass criterion: your position survives a peer asking "what would change your mind?" — you can name the specific observation that would flip you.

**Problem 3 — Teardown.** Pick one named AI category from 2024–2026 (AI SDR, AI customer support, AI legal, AI code editor, vibe coding, AI close-automation, AI medical scribe) and write a 600-word teardown: (a) who named the category, (b) what signals did they invest in (book, conference, podcast, content cadence), (c) how quickly did imitators arrive, (d) what was the category-king share of economics at the time of analysis, and (e) what specific positioning move would a new entrant in 2026 make to capture share without attacking the king head-on. Pass criterion: at least five specific, dated, cited facts from public sources.

**Problem 4 — Repositioning plan.** Design a 12-month repositioning plan for a scenario in which you must exit your current category (contaminated, saturated, or misaligned) and land in a stronger one. Your plan must specify: the new category label, the three competitive alternatives you'll anchor against, the unique attribute you'll build to justify the new category (not just rename), the content cadence you'll run to evangelize the new label (specific platform, specific frequency), and the kill criteria — what observation at month 6 would cause you to abort the repositioning and retreat.

**Problem 5 — Diagnose your harm.** Identify where your current positioning is doing you the most harm. Use the three harm signatures from Layer 5 (too-broad category, wrong alternatives setting your price, category contaminated by a bad-actor vendor) and commit to a specific revision within 30 days. Pass criterion: the revision changes at least three of the five Dunford components, not just the label.

## Common failure modes at scale

- **Renaming without rebuilding.** Changing the category label in your headline while keeping the same alternatives, attributes, and value proposition. Buyers notice the mismatch within one call. The fix: when you change the category, cascade the change through all five components and rewrite every piece of customer-facing collateral.
- **Evangelizing a category you are not the king of.** Publishing a book or running a conference for a category created by a better-capitalized vendor makes you their unpaid marketing arm. The fix: only evangelize categories where you are either the king or a named top-three player; for others, be a skilled operator, not a missionary.
- **Treating positioning as a launch artifact.** Writing the five components once, then never revisiting. Positioning should be re-audited every six months against the three harm signatures; markets reprice, categories contaminate, and the work is ongoing.
- **Conflating narrative with positioning.** A strong Raskin narrative without a strong Dunford structure sells a vibe but can't anchor a price. A strong Dunford structure without a strong Raskin narrative produces sales collateral no buyer reads. Build both, always.
- **Fighting the category king on their axis.** If you sell against Harvey in AI legal, you will lose. If you sell alongside Harvey in a sub-category Harvey does not prioritize (e.g., mid-market legal ops rather than AmLaw 100), you can win. Sub-categorize; don't contest.
- **Over-trusting category naming as a substitute for product.** 11x named its category competently and shipped a broken product; the category name could not carry the reputation once the product was exposed.[^14][^15] The category is a packaging move; the product must work.

## Open questions / what's not settled

**Is the 76% category-king share claim from *Play Bigger* actually replicable at AI-category scale?** The TTMC research covered 2000–2015 VC-funded markets, a period of roughly monolithic category structures.[^7][^8] AI categories in 2024–2026 are fragmenting faster — "AI SDR" had eight named vendors within 18 months, "AI code editor" has four, "AI legal" at least five. Whether 76% capture still holds, or whether modern AI categories produce flatter share distributions, is an empirical question that won't resolve until 2027–2028. An honest operator treats the number as a historical upper bound, not a current law.

**Does Raskin's strategic-narrative frame still work when every vendor is running an old-game/new-game script?** By 2026, the "old way vs. new way" structure has been commoditized by marketing content farms and LLM-generated sales-deck templates. Buyers may have pattern-matched to it and started to discount it. The counter-evidence: Drift, Gong, and Salesforce narratives still work; the frame is durable. The open question is whether a *new* frame will emerge to replace old-game/new-game before 2027.

**Can a solo operator create a category in 2026, or is it structurally a well-capitalized-company play?** Hamel Husain has effectively created a sub-category ("AI evals specialist") at operator scale; Andrej Karpathy created a lexical category ("vibe coding") with one tweet.[^12][^19] But both had pre-existing reputational capital (Husain at Airbnb, Karpathy at OpenAI/Tesla). Whether a genuinely unknown operator can create a category through sustained content alone, or whether categorical creation always requires some pre-existing distribution surface, is contested. The pragmatic position: assume pre-existing distribution is required and build it *before* attempting category creation, not during.

## Reviewer lens — named critics with specific disagreements

- **April Dunford** (obviouslyawesome.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning; *SaaS Half Full* podcast 2024, "It's a Crapshoot: Category Creation & Positioning")[^1][^10] would push back on Layer 3's synthesis that category creation is "correct strategy for a small number of companies and wrong strategy for most." Her 2024 position is sharper: she'd argue that *categories emerge; companies don't create them*, and that the lesson's framing implicitly endorses category creation as a choice when the base rate (fewer than 35 successes 2000–2015) suggests it is almost never the right choice for founders under-capitalized for a decade-long evangelism commitment. Specific counter-claim: replace "correct strategy for a small number of companies" with "correct strategy for a statistically negligible number of companies, and the rest should niche inside existing categories."

- **Christopher Lochhead** (*Play Bigger* book; *Christopher Lochhead Follow Your Different* podcast)[^7][^8][^11] would push back on Layer 5's repositioning protocol, which treats repositioning as a tactical cascade inside an existing category. His counter-claim, articulated in *Category Pirates* writing: repositioning inside an existing category is the definitional losing move because the category king has already captured 76% of the economics; the only repositioning worth doing is *out of* the existing category into a new one you define. Specific pushback: the harm signature "category is too broad" should prompt category creation, not category narrowing; narrowing concedes the frame to the incumbent.

- **Andy Raskin** (andyraskin.com; *Lenny's Podcast* 2023 on strategic narrative; Firm Narrative Medium archive)[^3][^4] would push back on Layer 1's framing of narrative as "downstream of positioning." His counter-position: without narrative, Dunford's five-component output is internal-alignment collateral that never reshapes the buyer's frame; the lesson treats narrative as a delivery vehicle when it is the frame that makes the positioning land. Specific counter-claim: the workflow should run narrative-first ("what old game is my buyer stuck in?") and positioning-second, not the other way around.

- **Hamel Husain** (hamel.dev; Parlance Labs)[^19] would push back on Layer 4's teardown 5, which positions him as a sub-category creator. His implicit counter, visible across his blog: he did not set out to create a sub-category; he set out to solve a specific technical problem (LLM eval infrastructure) for a specific buyer, and the sub-category emerged as a retrospective label. Specific counter-claim: the lesson's framing risks prescribing "sub-category creation" as a strategy when in practice it is an emergent outcome of solving a narrow technical problem exceptionally well; operators who optimize for the label rather than the work will over-position and under-deliver.

- **Bob Moesta** (*Learning to Build*; JTBD school)[^20] would push back on the entire framing of positioning and category creation as independent of job-to-be-done analysis. His counter: the Dunford "best-fit customer" and "value" components are downstream of a JTBD analysis that the lesson does not make explicit; without knowing the job the buyer is hiring the offer to do, you cannot legitimately name the alternatives (the alternatives are whatever they currently hire to do the job) or the value (whatever outcome metric they judge the job by). Specific counter-claim: the lesson should include a prerequisite step of JTBD analysis feeding into the Dunford components; otherwise the five components are hypothesis-generating, not hypothesis-validating.
