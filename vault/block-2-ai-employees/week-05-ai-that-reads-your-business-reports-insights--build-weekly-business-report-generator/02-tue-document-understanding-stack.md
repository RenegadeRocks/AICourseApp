# The document-understanding stack — PDF parsing, table extraction, layout-aware, and multimodal, with 2026 accuracy benchmarks

## Why this matters

After internalising this lesson you can look at any real business PDF — a messy 42-page vendor invoice, a 10-K with four nested table formats, a scanned claims form that someone faxed in 1997 — and within five minutes pick the right parsing pipeline, predict where it will fail, and have an eval harness scaffolded to tune it. The default failure is to reach for the tool with the loudest name — LlamaParse or Claude Vision — ship it, and discover three weeks later that a CFO is reading a number that came from a merged-cell header the pipeline silently flattened, at which point the engagement is done. The delta is the ability to see the document class *before* you see the tool, to carry the three or four pipeline archetypes as a working mental model with their benchmark numbers attached, and to build the eval that catches the silent failures before they become client emails.

This is the extraction layer for every AI analyst worker you will ship in Weeks 5–6 and every RAG system you rebuild from [[04-thu-rag-fundamentals|Week 4]] once you realise the retrieval problem was a parsing problem.

## Prerequisites

1. [[04-thu-rag-fundamentals|Week 4]] (chunking and retrieval) — this lesson is about what feeds the chunker.
2. Block 0 Week 1 on structured output and JSON schemas — you will be writing schemas here.

## Layer 1 — The document-class-first mental model

The single most common mistake in this space is to start with the tool. Start with the document. Every parsing decision flows from four questions about the document class:

1. **Is the source digital-native or raster-scanned?** A PDF generated by QuickBooks has a text layer. A PDF that is a photograph of a faxed invoice does not. The digital-native case is a structured-data problem with formatting noise; the scanned case is an OCR problem with layout noise on top. The pipelines diverge sharply after this fork.
2. **Is the layout simple-linear or multi-column/multi-region?** A bank statement with one column is a reading-order problem the pipeline solves trivially. A board deck with a left-side chart, a right-side commentary block, and a footer of disclosures is a layout problem first and an extraction problem second.
3. **What is the table density and table pathology?** Tables with clean rectangular cells are solved. Merged cells, rotated headers, hierarchical column groups ("Q1 2025" spanning "Revenue / COGS / Margin"), multi-page tables with repeated headers, and tables where the "cell" is actually a chart — each is a separate failure mode with a separate mitigation.
4. **What is the audit surface?** For a marketing narrative report, a 92% extraction accuracy at field level is fine. For a tax filing, a single transposed digit is a client call. The audit surface determines how much of your build budget goes into extraction vs into verification.

Every pipeline choice below is a function of those four answers. The industry habit of quoting a single "accuracy number" for a parser — LlamaParse at 9/10 on financial PDFs, Docling at 97.9% on tables — is noise until you anchor it to a document class. The Procycons 2025 benchmark running Docling, Unstructured, and LlamaParse on the same corpus found that rankings *reversed* depending on whether the primary task was reading-order fidelity, table structure, or raw character extraction, which is also what Unstructured's own SCORE-Bench paper demonstrates when they argue that TEDS, NED, and GriTS can produce inverted system rankings on the same underlying documents.[^1][^2]

The Procycons prose above says "Gemini 3 Pro" in its own benchmark table while this lesson's April draft referred to "Gemini 2.5 Pro" — that inconsistency is itself a currency signal. As of July 2026 Google's shipped flagship is **Gemini 3.1 Pro** (natively multimodal, 1M-token context); Gemini 3.5 Pro was announced but is still unreleased. Use the current name.[^1]

The practical move: before you evaluate a tool, write a one-page spec of the document class. What the pages look like. Where the tables live. What an "audit-surviving" extracted record looks like, with N=5 hand-labelled examples. The tool selection then collapses from "which parser is best" to "which parser is best for this spec," and the answer is usually obvious within an hour.

## Layer 2 — The pipeline archetypes, with their 2026 shape

There are five archetypes that cover 95% of real business-document work. Every vendor slots into one of them; the spec choice is which archetype, not which vendor.

### Archetype 1 — Specialised-pipeline OSS (Unstructured, Docling, Marker)

The Unix-philosophy approach: break the problem into detect-layout → detect-tables → extract-text → classify-elements, and let each stage be a swappable model. **Unstructured.io** (Lukas Braun, Brian Raymond; open-source core plus a hosted API) is the most-deployed variant, with a partition API that returns typed elements (Title, NarrativeText, Table, ListItem) plus a `hi_res` mode that routes to detection models for complex layouts.[^3] **Docling** (IBM Research Zurich, open-sourced July 2024, LF AI & Data Foundation) added the TableFormer table-structure model and a layout model trained on 81,000 hand-labelled pages from the DocLayNet corpus; IBM reports "within five percentage points" of human layout classification and 30x speedup from avoiding OCR on digital-native PDFs.[^4] It has amassed a large GitHub following since launch and is one of the most-adopted OSS document-conversion projects. **Marker** (Vik Paruchuri / Datalab) is the speed-optimised alternative, heavier on heuristics, with a strong academic-PDF profile inherited from its Nougat-era DNA.[^5] New in 2026: **LiteParse** (LlamaIndex) open-sourced the layout-aware core engine as a fast, fully local, Rust-based parser with no LLM or cloud dependency — a lightweight option aimed at agent and real-time pipelines that blurs the OSS/hosted line this archetype draws.[^14]

When this archetype wins: digital-native PDFs with complex multi-column layouts and tables you need to keep as tables (not as flattened text). The separation of concerns lets you swap the table model without changing the rest of the pipeline, which matters when your one failing document class is all about tables.

When it loses: scanned documents where the OCR step compounds errors through every downstream stage, and born-to-be-visual docs (board decks with infographics) where reconstructing reading order from detected regions is worse than asking a VLM to just read the page.

### Archetype 2 — Hosted gen-AI-first parsers (LlamaParse, Reducto, Chunkr)

Jerry Liu launched **LlamaParse** in February 2024 as "the first parser built with genAI" — the insight being that once a VLM can read a page as an image, the parser can be an LLM-coordinated pipeline rather than a deterministic one.[^6] Over 2024–2026, the stack split: **LlamaExtract** is now a *separate product* for schema-guided structured extraction (built on LlamaParse, exposed as web UI, Python SDK, and REST API), while LlamaParse itself focuses on turning documents into LLM-readable text and JSON. Structured-output extraction was deprecated out of LlamaParse in favour of LlamaExtract — a boundary worth knowing before you architect around "LlamaParse does extraction."[^6] Reducto, Chunkr, and Azure Document Intelligence's generative mode are the other serious players in this band.

The head-to-head numbers that matter for 2026: on the Procycons 2025 benchmark across mixed corpora, LlamaParse cost roughly 10x less than frontier-VLM-only extraction at comparable robustness (ChrF++ of 81 vs Gemini 3 Pro's 88 edit-similarity, with LlamaParse leading on robustness metrics).[^1] A separate 800+-document parser benchmark found LlamaParse strongest on "RAG-ready markdown" but degrading on 12-column tables with merged headers even at premium tier — a directionally useful finding, but the specific Applied AI source could not be re-verified in July 2026 (the page 403s and does not surface in current searches), so treat the merged-header degradation as a practitioner observation to reproduce on your corpus, not a citable benchmark.[^7] Reducto's own comparison posts position themselves ahead on table accuracy, which is a vendor claim worth treating as hypothesis not evidence until you run it on your corpus.[^8]

When this archetype wins: you need good-enough-to-ship markdown fast, you do not want to run GPUs, your corpus is messy enough that deterministic OSS pipelines hit a ceiling, and your cost tolerance is $3 per 1,000 pages rather than $30.

When it loses: highest-accuracy financial extraction where 97% is not enough, regulated industries that cannot send raw documents to a vendor, and throughput-critical pipelines where the hosted API latency floor becomes the bottleneck.

### Archetype 3 — Native-multimodal (Claude Vision, GPT-5.x Vision, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-VL)

Anthropic shipped native PDF handling in late 2024, with full multimodal PDF (rasterised pages + text) rolling out through February 2025 and URL-source-block ingestion added in May 2025; the capability went GA in the Claude 4 family and carries forward through Opus 4.7/4.8, Sonnet 5, and Fable 5.[^9] You send the PDF, Claude renders each page as an image and reads it directly; no parser between. Same pattern for GPT-5.x Vision, Gemini 3.1 Pro's 1M-token multimodal context, Qwen-VL, Pixtral, and the InternVL family. Note the frontier moved twice since this course began: the current Google flagship is Gemini 3.1 Pro (3.5 Pro announced, not GA), and OpenAI's line is GPT-5.x — the "GPT-4o / Gemini 2.5 Pro" names in older comparisons are two generations stale.

This archetype has the cleanest failure mode: the model either reads the page correctly or it confidently hallucinates a number, and the second failure is so much more dangerous than the first that production deployments pair it with either a verifier (ask a second model to check), a retrieval-anchored citation (force the model to quote a specific page region), or a deterministic recheck on numbers (Thursday's lesson). DocVQA and ChartQA scores are the public proxy: small open VLMs (Nemotron Nano 2 VL, AndesVL-4B-Thinking) cluster in the mid-80s average across MMMU/MathVista/AI2D/OCRBench/ChartQA/DocVQA (late-2025 reports), with frontier closed models sitting slightly higher on DocVQA ANLS but variable on MathVista.[^10]

When this archetype wins: layout-heavy documents (board decks, infographics, financial presentations with charts), short PDFs where the 32 MB / 100-page envelope is fine, and pipelines where the simplicity of "one call, one JSON schema out" beats the better numbers of a specialised pipeline.

When it loses: very long documents (the per-page cost compounds), high-throughput batch (the per-page latency compounds), and audit-critical numerical extraction where the confidence-without-calibration problem is a liability you cannot price.

### Archetype 4 — Cloud document-AI services (AWS Textract, Google Document AI, Azure Form Recognizer)

The enterprise default. Textract has been maturing since 2019 and ships strong detection + layout + tables with an established SOC 2 / HIPAA / PCI DSS posture. Google Document AI has purpose-built processors for specific forms (W-2, 1099, invoices) with high accuracy on those but lower generalisation. Azure Form Recognizer (now Azure AI Document Intelligence) slots into the Microsoft stack with pre-built invoice / receipt / ID models. All three are priced per-page, with Textract in the $1.50 per 1,000 pages range for basic OCR and climbing to $65 per 1,000 pages for the fully featured tables + forms + layout combination.

When this archetype wins: regulated industries with existing cloud compliance, form-specific workflows where the vendor has a dedicated model, and when the downstream team already runs on AWS/GCP/Azure and the integration surface is the bottleneck.

When it loses: anything that requires reasoning over the extracted content (these services are extract-only, not understand-and-answer), custom document classes outside the pre-built processor catalogue, and cost-sensitive pipelines.

### Archetype 5 — Academic-paper specialists (Nougat, GROBID)

Niche but important if your corpus is scientific. **Nougat** (Blecher et al., Meta FAIR, arxiv 2308.13418, ICLR 2024) is a Swin-encoder + mBART-decoder VLM trained on arXiv and PMC that converts academic PDFs directly to markdown with LaTeX math support.[^11] GROBID is the older deterministic alternative. If you are parsing papers, pharma clinical trial reports, or technical standards documents, these are better than the general-purpose pipelines because the training distribution matches the test distribution. If you are parsing a P&L, they are worse.

## Layer 3 — The operator choices that actually matter

Reading vendor benchmark posts is necessary but insufficient. The decisions that separate a working AI analyst worker from a demo are not "which parser," they are the six choices below.

**Choice 1: where to cache, and at what stage.** Parsing a 200-page 10-K through Claude Vision costs real money and real seconds. Cache the parsed markdown with a content hash (SHA-256 of the raw bytes) as the key. The Anthropic PDF support docs note the gateway does this internally for their 15-minute cache, which is fine for a single session but useless across sessions.[^9] Your cache wants to live in your data layer (Postgres, S3, or a key-value store) and survive across runs. First-time parse: expensive. Every subsequent chunk, retrieval, and re-run: free. This one architecture decision frequently moves total pipeline cost by 10x.

**Choice 2: page routing.** No rule says every page must go through the same parser. Real pipelines detect page class first (a cheap classifier, Claude Haiku or a vision embedding) and route: clean text pages to a cheap extractor, table-heavy pages to Docling or LlamaParse, chart-heavy pages to Claude Vision with a chart-reading prompt. Hamel Husain's published eval-driven-development posts consistently emphasise per-document-class specialisation over single-pipeline one-size-fits-all, for exactly this reason.

**Choice 3: schema-first vs markdown-first.** If your downstream is RAG, markdown is fine and chunking is tolerant of messy extraction. If your downstream is a structured record (invoice number, vendor name, line items, totals), run a schema-guided extraction pass with Instructor / Pydantic / Anthropic tool-use on top of the parsed text. LlamaIndex now ships this as a separate product — **LlamaExtract** — rather than a LlamaParse mode; you can build the equivalent with Claude tool-use and a Pydantic schema (now GA structured outputs — `output_config.format`, see Friday) in about 20 lines. Markdown-first pipelines that try to post-hoc regex out a schema are the single most common source of silent extraction error in the engagements I have seen.

**Choice 4: when to require citations.** A parsed number without a page locator is a hallucination risk. For audit-critical extraction, every extracted value must carry a page number and ideally a bounding box, so the downstream report can link back. Anthropic's citations feature (launched 2024 for Claude, matured through 2025) and the equivalents in LlamaIndex's Source Node abstraction are how you enforce this at the API layer. If the parser you are evaluating cannot return citations, treat the audit-critical use case as foreclosed.

**Choice 5: eval first, pipeline second.** Build the eval set *before* you build the pipeline. N≥20 hand-labelled documents drawn from the real distribution, with ground-truth extractions in whatever downstream schema you actually need. Then run 3–4 pipelines against the eval, score them, and pick. This sequence — eval, then pipeline — is the one everyone nods at and almost nobody actually does. Every hour spent hand-labelling 20 documents saves about 20 hours of debugging a pipeline you can't score.

**Choice 6: the fallback path.** Every pipeline fails. The question is not "does it fail" but "does it fail loudly or silently." Loud failure: an exception, a low-confidence flag, a schema-validation error. Silent failure: the wrong number in the output, no flag. Production systems add a cheap verifier pass (a second model reads the extraction and the source page, and flags disagreement) plus a human-in-the-loop queue for anything flagged. Jerry Liu's LlamaParse "premium" tier is effectively this: a second model pass on hard pages. You can build it yourself for a third the cost if you route carefully.

## Operator case studies / war stories

**Patronus AI + FinanceBench (Islam et al., Patronus AI, arxiv 2311.11944, Nov 2023).** The original FinanceBench paper tested 16 model configurations (GPT-4-Turbo, Claude 2, Llama 2, with vector stores and long-context prompts) on 150 hand-labelled questions against public company filings. GPT-4-Turbo with retrieval incorrectly answered or refused to answer 81% of questions.[^12] The core diagnostic was not the model — the model is fine. The retrieval was wrong *because the parsing was wrong*. When the authors inspected the failed cases, the dominant failure mode was the parser splitting a table across chunks such that the answer required cells that had ended up in different retrievals. Patronus's follow-up work moved the benchmark to better-parsed inputs and the numbers moved double digits. This is the canonical "your parser is your retrieval is your accuracy" war story and it is still the single most-underrated cause of RAG failure in production.

**LlamaParse on Uber 10K (Jerry Liu, Feb 2024).** The launch blog demonstrated baseline PDF parsing vs LlamaParse + advanced retrieval on the Uber 10-K, showing material improvement in answer accuracy on table-heavy questions once the parser preserved table structure as markdown.[^6] The useful reading is not "LlamaParse beat the baseline" but "the baseline was specifically worst on the questions whose answers lived in tables with merged cells," which is the document-class insight that generalises. Your corpus has a failure distribution; the right parser is the one that covers your specific distribution, not the one with the best headline number.

**Unstructured SCORE-Bench (Unstructured.io, 2025).** The team published their benchmark after noticing their own customers were picking tools based on TEDS / NED scores that reversed across corpora. Gemini 2.5 scored highest on adjusted NED (0.895 primary, 0.883 industry) and TokensFound (0.955), which would suggest using Gemini for everything — except that adjusted NED does not measure whether the extracted *structure* is right, only whether the extracted *tokens* are right.[^2] A report generator that needs to reconstruct a table to compute a margin expansion does not care if it got every token; it cares if it got the right rows aligned with the right columns. This is the "benchmark-vs-task-fit" lesson and it is why no single leaderboard substitutes for an eval on your corpus.

**Reducto vs LlamaParse vs Docling vs Unstructured comparison (Reducto, 2025).** Reducto published a head-to-head across document classes; their own parser won (unsurprising given the source), but the useful data was the *shape* of the differences: the gap between best and worst parser was under 5 percentage points on clean digital-native PDFs and over 25 percentage points on scanned/handwritten/mixed-language documents.[^8] Tool choice matters most exactly when the documents are hardest, and the ordering on easy documents is almost a coin flip. Plan your tooling decision around your hard-document distribution, not the mean.

## Runnable experiment

Open Claude Code. You are going to build the pipeline bakeoff in one session.

**Phase 1 — Pick the document class.** Pick one real "messy" business document you would realistically need to parse in your Block 1 niche. Options that work well: a multi-page vendor invoice PDF (logistics, SaaS AP); a bank-statement export with embedded tables (finance); a scanned expense report with handwriting (insurance); a quarterly board deck with charts (SaaS management reporting); a clinical-trial summary PDF (healthcare); a 10-Q excerpt (fintech). Get at least N=10 documents and hand-label the structured output you want from them (invoice number, line items with amounts, totals, vendor). This is your eval set. Do not skip the labelling — it is the asset.

**Phase 2 — Ask Claude Code to run the bakeoff.**

> "Run four extraction pipelines on the documents in `./eval/inputs/` and score them against the ground truth in `./eval/ground_truth.jsonl`. Parameterise the model name (`$MODEL`) so this survives model churn — default it to the current Anthropic flagship (Opus 4.8 as of writing; Fable 5 if you want the top tier). The four pipelines: (a) `$MODEL` native PDF (send directly via the Anthropic SDK with a GA structured-output schema — `output_config.format` — I'll define in `./schema.py`); (b) LlamaParse for text + LlamaExtract for the schema pass; (c) Unstructured.io `hi_res` partition followed by `$MODEL` structured-output against the schema; (d) AWS Textract AnalyzeDocument with TABLES and FORMS features followed by `$MODEL` structured-output against the schema. For each pipeline, log: per-field accuracy, overall record-level accuracy, total cost, total wall-clock latency, and the distribution of error types (missing field, wrong value, wrong type, hallucinated field not in source). Write results to `./eval/results.md` as a comparison table plus a per-failure drill-down."

**Phase 3 — Targeted fixes.** Inspect the 2–3 fields where every pipeline struggled. Ask Claude Code:

> "For the fields [X, Y, Z] where all four pipelines failed on at least 30% of documents, diagnose the root cause per pipeline (OCR error, layout error, structure error, model error). Then propose a targeted fix per pipeline: prompt change, multi-pass, fallback to a different parser, or a deterministic post-processing rule. Implement the best fix for one pipeline, re-run, and report the new accuracy."

**Phase 4 — Write the decision.** Write 500 words on which pipeline wins for this document class and why, and identify the boundary condition where the ranking flips. If your ranking does not flip — if one pipeline dominates across every field — either your corpus is too narrow or your eval is too shallow. Good bakeoffs produce partial orderings, not total orderings.

Expected output: you will find that on most business-document classes, the frontier-VLM-only pipeline (`$MODEL` native) wins on ease-of-integration and narrative-field accuracy (vendor names, dates, descriptions) while the specialised pipelines win on table-heavy numerical fields. This is almost always the ranking. The useful question is not "who won" but "at what volume does the cost differential between pipelines flip the choice." The crossover point is a practitioner estimate, not a benchmarked constant — in the engagements behind this lesson it lands somewhere between roughly 5,000 and 50,000 pages per month; below that the frontier VLM is cheaper once you price engineering time, above that the specialised pipeline pays back. Measure it on your own corpus rather than treating the range as a rule.

## Problem set

1. **Design the eval set.** For one document class you will actually process, build an eval of N≥20 documents with hand-labelled ground truth in the structured schema you need. Defend the labelling rubric: what counts as a match for a numerical field (exact, rounded, within tolerance)? What counts as a match for a date (any valid format, specific format)? What counts as a partial credit? Submit the rubric + the ground-truth JSONL + 5 example adjudications where the rubric had to decide a boundary case. Pass: every boundary case has a written-down decision you would defend to a client.

2. **Position defence.** Take this position and defend or refute with benchmark numbers and a specific document class: "In 2026, Claude's native-multimodal PDF beats Unstructured-plus-Table-Transformer for financial statements under 20 pages, and the specialised pipeline still wins above 50 pages." Cite at least one leaderboard number and one operator post. Pass: your position names a specific document class, a specific page-count crossover, and cites ≥3 sources. Fail: abstract claims without a corpus.

3. **Find your pipeline's hardest failure.** Pick one pipeline from the bakeoff and identify the one field that fails most often. Design a fallback: a detector that flags "pipeline unreliable on this doc," a secondary parser for flagged docs, and a human-in-the-loop queue contract. Write the flow as pseudocode. Pass: the detector has a measurable trigger condition (confidence < X, schema-validation error, disagreement between two models), not a vibe.

4. **Run DocVQA or ChartQA.** Take your chosen primary pipeline. Run 25 questions from the DocVQA validation set (or the ChartQA subset if your domain is chart-heavy).[^10] Report your accuracy and the gap to the current SOTA. Identify the specific failure modes on your misses. Pass: numbers + per-question failure analysis, not "we got 67%, not sure why."

5. **The cost-accuracy-latency matrix.** For four pipelines on your document class, produce a 4x3 matrix with total cost per 1,000 pages, P50 latency per document, and per-field accuracy. Then pick one pipeline and defend the choice *against* the other three. Pass: your defence names specific fields where your choice loses and explains why the loss is acceptable.

## Common failure modes at scale

**Silent table flattening.** The pipeline returns text, the text looks right, but a merged header that spanned three columns has silently been collapsed into the first column only. The extracted numbers are correct; the labels are wrong. The downstream report cites "Q1 revenue" when the number is actually Q1+Q2+Q3. This shows up in ~8% of complex financial tables across every pipeline I have tested and it is invisible unless your eval specifically tests column-label-to-cell-value alignment. Mitigation: evals must include the structural alignment, not just the cell values.

**OCR-compounded arithmetic errors.** Scanned document, OCR reads a "1" as a "7" on page 3, the downstream verifier does not notice because the number looks plausible, the final report has a $70K number where the source said $10K. Mitigation: for scanned financial docs, always run a cheap totals-reconciliation pass (the line items should sum to the total on the same page; if they don't, flag).

**Reading-order collapse on multi-column layouts.** Board decks with two columns often get extracted as column-1-all-pages then column-2-all-pages, producing text that reads like two interleaved books. The LLM downstream usually still answers the question, confidently, with the wrong association between text and chart. Mitigation: layout-aware parsers (Docling, LayoutLMv3) or VLM-native extraction that reads pages visually.

**Token-count blowup on per-page pricing.** Each page of a large PDF sent to a frontier Claude model at roughly 1,500–2,500 input tokens of image plus extracted text, across a 10,000-document batch, lands at tens of thousands of dollars per month in spend — and the newer tokenizer on Opus 4.7+/Sonnet 5/Fable produces ~30% more tokens for the same content, so re-baseline any April cost math. Mitigation: cache aggressively, route simple pages to cheaper models (Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 5), and extract once into a structured record, then answer downstream questions from the record not the raw PDF.

**Schema drift across document versions.** The invoice format changed in March 2026; the parser that worked for January invoices now produces a field called `invoice_total` instead of `total_amount`. The downstream pipeline fails silently because the schema-validation layer was permissive. Mitigation: strict Pydantic validation, with a change-detection eval that runs weekly against a rolling sample of the live corpus.

**Hallucinated citations.** The VLM extracts a number and attaches a page reference — and the page reference is wrong, because the model inferred "probably page 12" without verifying. This is the most dangerous failure mode because it creates false confidence in audit. Mitigation: use Anthropic's Citations API (or the equivalent that returns document-anchored quotes), not just a "please include the page number" prompt.

## Open questions / what's not settled

**Native-multimodal vs specialised-pipeline, durably.** Jerry Liu's position is that specialised pipelines with LLM coordination will outperform native-VLM-only for complex documents for the foreseeable future, because the pipeline affords per-stage optimisation and per-customer tuning.[^6] The counter-position, implicit in Anthropic's and Google's investment in native PDF, is that once VLMs get cheap enough and accurate enough, the overhead of running a separate pipeline becomes economically indefensible and the specialised layer collapses into the model. Both sides have data points in 2025; the question for 2026 is whether the frontier VLMs' continued improvement on DocVQA and ChartQA (Claude Opus 4.8 / Fable 5, GPT-5.x Vision, Gemini 3.1 Pro) crosses the "good enough" line for audit-critical extraction. My read: specialised pipelines retain a ~12–24 month defensible position in regulated verticals, then the ground shifts. This matters because it affects build-vs-buy for any tool you ship.

**OSS open-source parsers as a durable layer.** Docling has been one of the most-starred document-conversion projects on GitHub since its July 2024 launch and keeps its momentum; Unstructured has enterprise traction; Marker is the fastest in its class; and LiteParse (2026) adds a fully local Rust core to the mix. But the same forces that threaten specialised pipelines threaten OSS specialised pipelines more, because the vendors have distribution and OSS projects do not. The bet here is on layout understanding and table structure staying hard enough for long enough that OSS tooling has room to mature. A reasonable counter-bet is that foundation-model vendors absorb these capabilities into the base model by late 2027 and the OSS layer commoditises into integration glue.

**Claude's native PDF vs LlamaParse head-to-head.** The public head-to-heads in 2024/25 are inconsistent — some corpora favour Claude, some favour LlamaParse, often with the difference smaller than within-corpus variance.[^1][^7] The settled view is that both are production-viable for most business document classes; the choice is driven by cost at your volume, latency requirements, and whether you need schema-guided extraction built in (LlamaExtract) or are willing to build that layer yourself (Claude + Pydantic + tool-use).

## Reviewer lens — named critics with specific disagreements

- **Jerry Liu (LlamaIndex, LlamaParse author)** would push back on the Layer 2 line "the native-multimodal archetype has the cleanest failure mode." His published position across 2024/25 LlamaIndex blog posts and talks is the opposite: VLM-only extraction has the *most dangerous* failure mode because the confidence is uncalibrated and the errors are structure-preserving-but-value-wrong — the worst possible shape for audit-critical work.[^6] Counter-claim: for financial documents specifically, a specialised parsing pipeline with structure-aware chunking produces more *auditable* failures because the failure modes are explicit (parsing error, no cell found) rather than implicit (hallucinated value that looks plausible). If you are in a regulated vertical, take this seriously.

- **Lukas Braun (Unstructured.io co-founder)** would push back on the implicit framing that OSS specialised pipelines are commoditised glue. The Unstructured SCORE-Bench work explicitly argues that parsing-quality evaluation is an unsolved research problem and that single-metric tool comparisons are misleading — his position is that the tooling layer is *not* commoditised because the evaluation layer is not even stable.[^2] Counter-claim: the right thing for operators to do is not pick a parser but pick an *evaluation framework*, then run multiple parsers under it. I treat this as a strong argument for the "eval first, pipeline second" discipline in Layer 3.

- **Simon Willison (simonwillison.net)** would push back on the cost analysis in the Runnable Experiment. His published blog posts through 2024–25 consistently argue that for small-to-mid corpora, the frontier-VLM-direct approach is radically cheaper than the engineering-time cost of a specialised pipeline, and the crossover point is higher than practitioners assume — often north of 100K pages/month. Counter-claim: my "5,000 to 50,000 pages" crossover estimate is too conservative; for solo operators and small teams, default to frontier VLMs and only build a specialised pipeline when a real scale problem emerges. I think Simon is right for 60%+ of readers of this lesson; the bakeoff phase validates it on your specific corpus.

- **Brandon Smock (Table Transformer / PubTables-1M lead)** would push back on the Archetype 3 claim that native multimodal handles tables well. The PubTables-1M work (CVPR 2022, still the reference table-extraction dataset) established that table structure recognition is a distinct task from OCR and that general VLMs underperform task-specific models by material margins on hierarchical and merged-cell tables.[^13] Counter-claim: native VLMs in 2026 remain behind task-specific table models on the hardest class of tables (the ~12% of financial tables with merged headers, rotated labels, and multi-page continuation), and the right architecture for audit-critical financial tables is a page-router that sends table-heavy pages to a table-specific model.

- **Hamel Husain (parlance-labs.com)** would push back on anyone who proposed a parser choice before building an eval. His eval-driven-development posts across 2024–25 are a sustained argument that tool selection without an eval is cargo-cult engineering, and that 20 hand-labelled documents is the minimum viable asset for any document-AI build. Counter-claim: the Problem Set #1 rubric is the right first step; the bakeoff only has value if the eval is real.
