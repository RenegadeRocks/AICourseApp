# Talking to humans — interviews that don't lie to you

## Why this matters

Everything before today was preparation for the only evidence source that cannot be homogenized, API-throttled, or hallucinated: a human being who has the problem, describing what they actually did about it. Interviews are also the evidence source easiest to corrupt, because the corrupting agent is you. Ask "would you use an AI that drafts your follow-ups?" and you will collect a warm stack of polite yeses that predict nothing; people answer hypothetical questions with their self-image and answer specific questions about last Tuesday with facts. Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* remains the definitive discipline for this, thirteen years on, and 2026 gives it new teeth in both directions: AI transcription and synthesis remove every logistical excuse for not interviewing, while an active wave of wiretap and biometric-privacy lawsuits against AI notetakers means the recording step now has legal footguns that did not exist when the book was written.[^1][^7][^8] Today you learn the discipline, recruit ten real prospects without having an audience, build a guide that cannot collect compliments, record legally, and synthesize without cherry-picking. Wednesday's output feeds Thursday's synthetic-user comparison and Saturday's ledger directly.

## Layer 1 — The Mom Test in three rules, tuned for agent products

Fitzpatrick's three rules, compressed:[^1]

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The interview is about their current workflow. Ideally your idea never comes up; when it must, it comes up last.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future.** "How do you handle follow-ups today? Walk me through the last one" beats every possible version of "would you...". Past behavior is data; predicted behavior is politeness.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** Every sentence you speak about your solution converts the interview from evidence collection into a pitch, and the data collected after a pitch is contaminated.

The classic corollary: compliments are the interview's failure mode, not its success signal. "That sounds great, I'd definitely use that" costs the speaker nothing. Fitzpatrick's currency test separates signal from noise: real interest pays in **time** (a scheduled follow-up, a workflow walkthrough with real data), **reputation** (an intro to a colleague with the same problem), or **money** (a pre-order, a paid pilot). No currency, no evidence.

For agent products specifically, add four probe families the book could not have anticipated:

- **The workaround excavation.** "What have you already tried?" The 2026 answer almost always includes an AI attempt. Follow it down: "You tried ChatGPT for this. What happened? Why did you stop?" The failure story of the horizontal default is the most valuable single data point you can collect, because it maps the exact gap your wedge must fill (Tuesday's alternatives-map row 3, now in first person).
- **The delegation boundary.** Agents absorb accountable work, so trust is the adoption variable. "Who checks this today? What's the worst version of it going wrong that you've actually seen?" You are locating the fine-you're-fired rung from Monday, and simultaneously the reliability bar your build would owe.
- **The frequency ledger.** "When did this last happen? And before that?" Two specific instances anchor a real frequency; "all the time" anchors nothing.
- **The budget archaeology.** Not "would you pay?" but "what does this cost you now?" and "have you ever paid anything to make this better?" People who have paid before pay again; people who have never paid for the pain rarely start with you.

## Layer 2 — Recruiting ten prospects without an audience

The honest hierarchy of recruiting channels, cheapest-credible first:

1. **Second-degree warm intros.** From Block 1 you have a niche map and, if you did the work, live relationships in it. The ask that converts: *"I'm researching how [role] handles [task]. Not selling anything. Could you point me to two people who deal with this weekly? 25 minutes, and I'll share back what I learn across all my conversations."* The learn-share-back offer matters: it makes you a peer running research, and it is an offer you can honor cheaply.
2. **Communities you already inhabit.** The forums and Discords you mined on Tuesday. Post the research ask openly (most communities tolerate research asks and punish disguised pitches; read the room and the rules). DM people whose complaint threads you read, referencing their own words: response rates on "I read your post about X, I'm researching exactly that" are an order of magnitude above cold generic outreach.
3. **Direct outreach, written by hand.** LinkedIn and email to people matching your customer definition. Two constraints from this vault's own current-events file: keep volume low and personal, and do not use AI-generated outreach copy on LinkedIn, which has been actively demoting it; hand-written, specific, short messages citing something true about the recipient.[^2] A 15–25% positive-reply rate on genuinely personal research asks (no pitch, easy scheduling, incentive offered) is achievable at this volume; if you are far below that, the message or the targeting is wrong, and that itself is evidence about reachability worth logging against your viability assumption.
4. **Paid recruiting panels.** When your buyer is genuinely outside your network: User Interviews (pay-as-you-go around $49 per B2C session and $98 per B2B session before incentives, with realistic all-in cost per completed interview in the $150–300 range once incentives are added) and Respondent (priced as ~50% of the participant incentive with a $40 minimum, strong for hard-to-reach professionals).[^3][^4][^5] Paid panels trade money for speed and add a screening burden: panel professionals exist, so screen for the behavioral specifics of your customer definition ("describe the last time you...") rather than titles.

Whatever the channel: schedule 10 to complete 5–7 (no-shows are structural), 25–30 minutes each, video call with camera on, and always end with the reputation-currency ask ("who else should I talk to?"), which compounds the panel while testing commitment.

**Incentives:** for peers-in-niche, the share-back memo usually suffices and keeps the conversation reciprocal. For strangers, pay the market rate ($50–100 for practitioners, $100–200 for executives, gift card or charity donation), because unpaid strangers who accept are self-selected for niceness, the exact trait that poisons your data.

## Layer 3 — The interview guide: five movements, thirty minutes

A guide is a route, not a script; follow the energy, but never skip a movement.

**Movement 1 — Context (3 min).** Who they are, what the surrounding workflow looks like. Confirms screening, warms the room.

**Movement 2 — The last time (10 min).** The core. "Walk me through the last time [task/problem] happened, start to finish." Then excavate: What triggered it? What tools were open? Where did it stall? Who else touched it? How long, end to end? Get *one full story in concrete detail* before generalizing. Then: "Is that typical? Tell me about a time it went badly."
**Movement 3 — Workarounds and spend (7 min).** What they have tried (including AI attempts, followed down), what it costs them now, what they have paid for, what they stopped paying for and why.

**Movement 4 — The stack rank (5 min).** "Of everything we discussed, what's the most annoying part? If a magic intern took exactly one piece of this off your plate, which piece?" Their prioritization, not yours. This is also where unexpected problems surface; some of your best pivots are sitting in this movement.

**Movement 5 — Currency close (5 min).** Only now, one or two sentences on what you are exploring, concrete and modest. Then the graduated asks: "Can I follow up in two weeks with what I build/learn?" (time), "Anyone else I should talk to?" (reputation), and, when the conversation has earned it, the pilot probe: "If I had a version that did [wedge] in four weeks, would you run it on your real workload as a paid pilot?" A yes here is scheduled, with a date, or it is a no with extra steps.

Every question in movements 1–4 must pass a mechanical filter before it enters the guide: *does it ask about the past or the present, and can the answer be a fact?* Perspective AI's 2026 question compendium is a competent public checklist if you want a swipe file to filter through the same test.[^6]

## Layer 4 — Recording, transcription, and consent: the 2026 rules

AI transcription changed interviewing economics: you can be fully present while the machine takes verbatim notes. It also picked up genuine legal risk. As of mid-2026, twelve US states (including California, Florida, Illinois, Washington, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, and Maryland) require **all-party consent** to record a conversation; Otter.ai is defending consolidated federal wiretap litigation over notetakers joining and recording meetings without adequate consent, and Fireflies.ai faces Illinois BIPA suits (Cruz v. Fireflies.AI Corp.) over voiceprint processing without informed written consent.[^7][^8][^9] The legal geometry, statutes, and interstate stricter-rule practice are canonical in [[04-thu-voice-agent-trust-and-safety|Block 3 Week 7 Thu]]; this lesson only ports the researcher's protocol:

1. **Affirmative spoken consent, on the recording.** Not a calendar-invite disclaimer, which multiple 2026 analyses flag as insufficient for all-party states.[^9] Script: *"I'd like to record this so I can be present instead of typing. The recording and an AI transcript stay with me, I'll anonymize anything I quote, and I'll delete the audio when the project ends. OK to start?"* Wait for the yes; it is now on tape.
2. **No autonomous notetaker bots.** Have your notetaker join with you and disclose it, or record natively in the meeting tool. A bot that joins meetings without you is precisely the fact pattern in the current lawsuits.[^7][^8]
3. **Data hygiene.** Transcripts into your project repo, names replaced with P1...P10 in anything that leaves your machine, audio deleted on a stated schedule. If the interviewee is in the EU, this is personal data; collect the minimum, honor deletion asks. Your consent script *is* part of your product research: the meeting-follow-up example product would inherit these exact obligations.
4. **Consent refusal is fine.** Take typed notes and flag the interview as notes-only in synthesis. Losing verbatim quotes is cheaper than losing trust, or a BIPA claim.

## Layer 5 — Synthesis without cherry-picking

The failure mode after ten interviews is anthological: you remember the three quotes that flattered the idea. The discipline that prevents it is the same one Hamel Husain teaches for LLM evals: look at *all* the data, tag it against a rubric fixed in advance, count.[^10] Concretely, and this is the exact schema Saturday's `note_synthesizer` tool automates:

1. **One evidence atom per claim.** Split each transcript into atoms: a specific statement, story, or commitment. One atom, one source, one quote.
2. **Tag each atom against the Monday assumptions:** which assumption it touches; direction (FOR / AGAINST / NEUTRAL); strength by evidence class (a specific past instance beats a general opinion; a currency commitment beats both; a compliment scores zero by rule).
3. **Count before you narrate.** "6 of 7 described the problem unprompted; 4 had built workarounds; 0 accepted the pilot probe" is a synthesis. "People were really excited" is a mood.
4. **Disconfirmation quota.** For every interview, extract *at least one* AGAINST or NEUTRAL atom before you are allowed to log any FOR atoms. Every conversation contains disconfirming material; a synthesis with none is measuring the synthesizer.
5. **LLM assistance with audit.** Claude is genuinely good at atom extraction and first-pass tagging across ten transcripts, and it inherits sycophancy: prompted naively it over-tags FOR. The tool's prompt (Saturday) forces per-atom verbatim quotes and instructs the model to prefer AGAINST when ambiguous; you audit every FOR atom above medium strength by reading its quote in context. Machine for recall, human for judgment: the same division of labor Thursday's protocol formalizes.

## Layer 6 — The live controversy: how many interviews is enough?

The book-era answer was "keep going until you stop hearing new things," commonly cited as dozens per segment, and modern practitioner guides still repeat 50–100 for major decisions.[^1][^11] The 2026 ship-school answer, met on Monday, is five-then-build, on the argument that build cost collapsed while interview cost did not, so the optimal evidence mix shifted.[^12] The NYU discovery-school reply is that interviews and shipping answer different questions, so they are complements, not substitutes on one dial.[^13]

The operational resolution this course teaches: **saturation within the wedge, not a magic number.** For a wedge as narrow as Monday forced yours to be, thematic saturation on the core desirability question typically arrives between interviews 5 and 10; when three consecutive interviews add no new atoms to the ledger's desirability rows, stop and escalate to a higher-cost evidence class (Friday's smoke tests, a paid pilot). What is *not* defensible at either pole: stopping at five when the five disagree, or scheduling interview twenty-three because interviews feel safer than shipping. Your pre-registered decision rule, not your comfort, decides.

## Worked example — one interview, atomized

From a hypothetical transcript with P3 (founder, 6-person ops consultancy), five minutes of Movement 2–3:

> "Last Thursday I had four client calls back to back. The Acme follow-up went out Monday morning... too late, honestly, they'd already emailed asking. [ATOM 1: desirability FOR, specific past instance, strength 4: follow-up lag with named consequence.] I have Fireflies on every call but the summary just sits in the app. [ATOM 2: desirability FOR, workaround exists and underperforms, strength 3.] I tried a Zapier thing to push notes into HubSpot, ran for a month, kept mis-filing, turned it off. [ATOM 3: desirability FOR + feasibility AGAINST, strength 4: demand receipt AND a reliability bar my wedge must clear.] Honestly the drafting isn't the hard part, I like writing the client emails myself, it's the CRM fields and the task handoffs that rot. [ATOM 4: wedge AGAINST, strength 4: my proposed wedge centers drafting; his pain centers structured updates. Pivot signal.] Would I pay? I mean, maybe, depends what it costs. [ATOM 5: viability NEUTRAL, strength 0: unprompted hedge, no currency.]"

Note what tagging did: a conversation that *felt* encouraging produced two atoms (4 and 5) that push against the current wedge and price confidence. Multiply by ten interviews and the ledger, not your memory, is deciding.

## Runnable experiment — guide, mock, and first real interview (2–3 hours across the day)

**Step 1 (30 min).** Draft your guide in the five-movement structure. Run the mechanical filter over every question; delete or rewrite any question about the future or about your idea.

**Step 2 (20 min).** Mock interview #1, adversarial: have Claude play your customer *badly*, with this framing: *"Play [customer definition]. Be polite, busy, and mildly interested in everything. Offer compliments and vague enthusiasm. Do not volunteer specifics unless asked about concrete past events."* Your job: extract two specific past instances and one currency signal from a respondent optimized to give you neither. This is interview calisthenics, and note the epistemics: the simulated customer is training your *technique*; nothing it says enters the ledger. Thursday explains exactly why that line is drawn where it is.

**Step 3 (20 min).** Mock interview #2 with a friend or cohort peer about a *real* workflow of theirs, full consent script and recording pipeline included, as a dry run of the tooling.

**Step 4 (rest of day / this week).** Send 15–20 recruiting messages across channels 1–3 (channel 4 if needed). Schedule ≥5. Complete ≥1 real interview today or tomorrow, recorded with spoken consent, transcript filed as `validation/interviews/P1.md`.

**Pass bar:** (a) guide contains zero hypothetical-future questions and zero idea exposition before Movement 5; (b) mock #1 yields two specific past instances extracted despite the compliment barrage; (c) recruiting messages sent with a tracked count (this number feeds Saturday's sprint math); (d) one real interview completed and atomized into ≥5 tagged atoms including ≥1 AGAINST/NEUTRAL.

## Common mistakes experts see

1. **Pitching in minute two.** The moment they know what you hope is true, every subsequent answer is contaminated. Idea last, or never.
2. **Accepting compliments as data.** "Sounds awesome" scores zero by rule. Only past behavior and currency count.
3. **Interviewing only friendlies.** Your niche's warm layer likes you; that liking leaks into the data. Ensure ≥3 of 10 interviews come from beyond your first-degree warmth.
4. **The calendar-invite consent fiction.** "This call may be recorded" in an invite body is not affirmative consent in all-party states, and the current lawsuits turn on exactly this gap.[^8][^9]
5. **Generalizing from "all the time."** Frequency claims without two dated instances are opinions. Excavate or discount.
6. **Synthesis by highlight reel.** Reading transcripts to find support is confirmation bias with a paper trail. Atomize everything, tag everything, count.
7. **Skipping the currency close out of politeness.** The ask is the assay. An interview that ends without any commitment request has measured conversation, not demand.
8. **Letting the notetaker attend alone.** Autonomous bots in meetings without disclosure are this year's litigation fact pattern.[^7]

## Open questions — what's not settled

**1. Do people tell machines different truths?** The AI-moderated-interview vendors' implicit claim is that a neutral machine interviewer removes the politeness bias humans trigger. There is real supporting literature (people disclose stigmatized information more readily to systems they believe aren't judging them) and real opposing intuition (rapport is what earns the second-layer story behind the first answer, and rapport is precisely what machines lack). The 2026 evidence base is thin on the question that matters for validation specifically: do prospects reveal *purchase-relevant* truths (real budgets, real internal politics, real switching costs) differently to an AI moderator? Nobody has published a convincing controlled comparison. Until someone does, treat AI moderation as a scale tool whose bias profile is unknown, and keep your highest-stakes conversations human.

**2. Is saturation measurable, or a feeling with a name?** This lesson's stopping rule (three consecutive interviews adding no new atoms) makes saturation operational, but the operationalization smuggles in a unit problem: atoms are researcher-defined, so a lazy tagger saturates early and a fine-grained one never does. Qualitative methodology has debated saturation metrics for decades without convergence, and the founder version inherits the debate at smaller n. The honest position: the three-interview rule is a discipline for stopping, not a theorem about completeness, and its real function is to force the escalation decision (to behavioral evidence) onto the calendar.

**3. Where does consent law go next?** The Otter and Fireflies litigation is unresolved; rulings could land anywhere from "disclosure banners suffice" to "biometric consent required per participant per meeting," and the EU AI Act's August 2026 full applicability adds transparency obligations whose interview-research implications are untested.[^8][^9] The conservative protocol this lesson teaches (affirmative spoken consent, no autonomous bots, minimal retention) is currently above every proposed bar, which is exactly where you want a practice you are also selling to clients: compliance headroom is a feature.

## Reflection questions

1. Which of your guide's questions are you secretly hoping gets a specific answer? What would a Mom-Test-compliant replacement look like, and why is the difference epistemically load-bearing?
2. Your warmest recruiting channel produces your most biased sample. How will you weight atoms from warm vs. cold interviews in the ledger, and can you justify the weights before seeing the data?
3. P3's ATOM 4 (drafting is fine, CRM rot is the pain) contradicts your wedge. At what atom count does "interesting signal" become "mandated pivot" under your Monday decision rule?
4. Construct the strongest argument that the ship-school is right *for your specific idea*: what would five interviews plus a two-week build learn that ten interviews cannot? Now the reverse. Which argument moved you more, and why?
5. Your consent script promises anonymization and deletion. If your product itself records meetings, what did writing that script teach you about your product's own trust requirements?
6. What question did your first real interviewee answer that you failed to ask? (There is always one. Finding it is how guides improve.)

## My take (reviewer lens)

**Michael Seibel** would bless the discipline and attack the ceremony: five movements, atom schemas, disconfirmation quotas... his version is "get them talking about the problem, shut up, and try to take money," and he would warn that a reader can hide from the scary part (the currency close, the recruiting DMs) inside the comfortable part (guide engineering). He is right that the asks are the assay; the apparatus exists to make the asks *interpretable*, not to defer them. **Hamel Husain** would sharpen Layer 5: tagging interview atoms is a labeling task, and labeling tasks need inter-rater checks; a solo founder tagging their own transcripts about their own idea is a single biased annotator, so at minimum re-tag a sample blind two days later, or swap transcripts with a cohort peer and compare tags. That is a genuinely better protocol than the lesson's solo version, and Saturday's tool supports it (its `--audit` sample exists for exactly this). **A cohort peer** would name the emotional truth the lesson under-weights: the first three cold recruiting messages are the hardest thing in this course so far, harder than any build, and the difference between readers who finish this week and readers who quietly stall is almost always whether those messages went out on Wednesday. Send them before you re-read anything.
