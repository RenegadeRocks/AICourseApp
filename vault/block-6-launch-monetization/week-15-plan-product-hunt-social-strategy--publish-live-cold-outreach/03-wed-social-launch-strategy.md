# Social launch strategy, the build-in-public engine and the launch-day content sequence

## Why this matters

Your launch day's outcome is mostly decided before launch day, by the audience and the warm list you assembled in the weeks prior (Monday, Layer 5). This lesson builds that engine, the build-in-public motion that pre-recruits the humans who give your launch its first-hour velocity, and then the launch-day content sequence that fires across LinkedIn and X against the 2026 algorithm reality. The stakes are concrete: a launch post to 800 genuinely interested followers, sequenced correctly, will out-produce a viral-looking post to 40,000 strangers on the only metric that matters, which is qualified signups. And in a feed where roughly 41% of LinkedIn long-form is now machine-written, a verifiably human founder voice is your cheapest competitive edge, well beyond a nicety, and the platform now mechanically rewards it. By the end you can run a pre-launch engine, script a five-post launch sequence, make a demo video that converts, and do all of it even if you are starting from near-zero followers.

## Layer 1: The build-in-public engine, as a launch instrument

Building in public, as a branding practice, is [[03-wed-building-in-public|Block 1 Week 2's]] subject, the receipts shelf, the content-as-moat debate, the creator-consultant trap. Here we use it for one narrow instrumental purpose: to pre-recruit the launch-day audience. The mechanism is simple and it is the whole reason pre-launch exists.

When you build in public for the weeks before launch — posting the problem you are solving, the decisions you are making, the ugly early screenshots, the "here's what broke today" — you are doing three things that pay off on launch day. First, you are **assembling a list of people who have agreed to care**: everyone who commented, replied, or asked to be told when it ships. Second, you are **manufacturing the origin story** in real time, so that on launch day the founder-story post writes itself from receipts rather than from marketing. Third, you are **earning the first-hour velocity** that every surface's algorithm uses to decide the rest of the day; those pre-committed humans are who show up in the first hour on Product Hunt, on your LinkedIn launch post, in the community thread.

The operator error is to build in public as a vague brand activity with no launch date attached. The launch-instrument version is time-boxed and list-building: for the four-to-six weeks before launch, every post has an implicit CTA — *"I'm shipping this on [date]; reply and I'll make sure you're the first to try it."* You are not performing; you are pre-selling a specific event to a specific list.

**The authenticity controversy, named.** There is a real and unresolved debate about whether building in public in 2026 is authentic founder transparency or performative theater, and you should hold a position. The skeptical side — voiced across Indie Hackers and Hacker News since 2024 — argues that when everyone builds in public, no one does: the practice has calcified into a genre with its own clichés (the vulnerable "here's my MRR" post, the manufactured setback-and-recovery arc), audiences have learned to discount it, and some founders are "trapped" maintaining an audience instead of building the product.[^1][^2] The believer side argues that the genre's fatigue is precisely why *genuine* transparency now stands out more, and that the receipts (real numbers, real failures, real decisions) still do the diligence-collapsing work [[03-wed-building-in-public|Block 1 Week 2]] documented. **My position:** the fatigue is real for *performed* building-in-public, the manufactured-vulnerability genre, and the edge is real for *substantive* building-in-public, where you show the actual work and name actual limitations. The distinction is the same one that governs the whole week: theater gets discounted, substance gets rewarded, and in a feed that is 41% machine-written the reward for substance is now mechanical, not just reputational.[^3] Build in public by showing the real thing, not by performing the genre.

## Layer 2: The 2026 algorithm constraints, as launch design inputs

You know the mechanics from [[02-tue-content-strategy-and-platform-choice|Block 1 Week 2]]. Here is how they *constrain the launch sequence specifically*, because a launch post that ignores them gets throttled at exactly the moment you most need reach.

**LinkedIn's anti-AI-slop demotion** actively suppresses generic AI-generated posts, AI comments, and content-automation tools out of recommendations, while stating that AI-*assisted* content with original thinking remains welcome.[^3] For your launch sequence this means: the posts must be verifiably in your voice, carry original specifics (real numbers, a real story, a real decision), and not read like the templated "🚀 Excited to announce…" launch-post genre the classifier is built to demote. The launch-post cliché is now a *reach penalty*, not just a taste failure.

**The external-link penalty** (both LinkedIn and X demote posts with links in the body) means the naive launch move — "we're live, click here" with the link in the post — costs you 30–50% of reach or more.[^4] The 2026-correct pattern: put the *substance* in the post itself, and the link in the *first comment* (LinkedIn) or a *reply to your own post* (X), then drive click-through from there. Your launch post teaches or tells a story; it does not lead with a link.

**X's ranker rewards conversation, not broadcast.** Replies dominate the signal, a two-way reply chain is weighted far above a like, and a per-author diversity cap means posting ten times dilutes rather than stacks your reach.[^5] For a launch this inverts the "blast it everywhere ten times" instinct: you want *fewer, stronger, reply-earning* posts, and then you spend launch day replying — to your own thread, to quote-tweets, to every comment, because the two-way reply chain is what the model amplifies. Launch day on X is a conversation you host, not an announcement you broadcast.

**X Premium confers a real distribution boost** (reported multiples in- and out-of-network), so if X is a Primary surface for your launch, holding Premium is part of the cost of the launch, not an optional extra.[^4]

The unifying design rule: on both surfaces, in 2026 you *earn reach with substance and conversation, then convert off-platform*. A launch sequence built on that rule works with the algorithms; one built on link-in-post broadcasting fights them and loses.

## Layer 3: The launch-day content sequence (five posts)

Here is the concrete sequence for launch day, designed to respect Layer 2 and to run whether your audience is 800 or 80,000. It is five posts spread across the day, not five posts fired at once (the diversity cap and the feed's attention window both punish clustering). Adapt the platform to where your buyer is; the structure holds for both.

**Post 1, the founder story (morning, at launch).** Not "we're live." The story of *why this exists*: the specific problem you lived, the moment you decided to build, what you learned making it. This is the post the build-in-public engine wrote for you in advance. It leads with the problem, carries real specifics, and ends with a soft "it's live today — reply and I'll send you access" (link in first comment). This post does the emotional and credibility work; it is the one most likely to travel because stories with real specifics clear the slop classifier and earn replies.

**Post 2, the demo (midday).** The product doing the thing, as a native video or GIF (Layer 4). Short, showing a *typical* run and honestly handling something hard. Caption teaches one insight — "the interesting part is how it handles [edge case]", so the post has value even to someone who never clicks. Native video is non-negotiable here: both platforms demote off-platform video links and reward native uploads, and video is the format buyers most want for "show me it works."[^6]

**Post 3, the proof / receipt (early afternoon).** A specific piece of evidence: an early user's result, a before/after metric, a screenshot of the product solving a real case, a quotable comment from the PH or community launch that is running in parallel. This is the receipts-shelf discipline ([[03-wed-building-in-public|Block 1 Week 2]]) deployed live. It converts the skeptics who found the story nice but want evidence.

**Post 4, the engagement/conversation post (afternoon).** A genuine question to your audience that invites the two-way replies X's ranker rewards and LinkedIn's engagement signal likes: "What's the most broken part of [the workflow you're solving]? I'm curious whether we built the right thing." This is not a launch announcement; it is a conversation that keeps you in the feed, surfaces objections you can answer publicly, and feeds every algorithm's conversation signal. Reply to every response substantively.

**Post 5, the thank-you / momentum recap (evening).** A genuine, specific thank-you to the people who showed up, with one honest note on how the day went (including what surprised you). This closes the loop, is a legitimate reason to appear in the feed again, and, because it names real people and real specifics — reads as human, not as a victory-lap template. It also seeds tomorrow: the humans who engaged today are your warm list and your interview pool.

Across all five, the operator's job between posts is the same as everywhere this week: *reply within the hour*, personally, to every comment and DM, because responsiveness is a ranked signal and a relationship at once. Five substantive posts plus real-time replies beats twenty templated posts, and it is the only pattern that works with the 2026 rankers rather than against them.

## Layer 4: Demo videos that convert

For an AI product, the demo video carries a disproportionate share of the conversion load, because the buyer's central question is "will it actually work, or is this a cherry-picked reel?" Wyzowl's 2026 data shows video's continued dominance in buyer research and conversion, and both social platforms now reward native video over any other format.[^6] The craft rules that matter for an AI-product demo specifically:

- **Show a typical run, not the best run.** Hamel Husain's warning from Tuesday applies here with force: a technically-literate audience assumes your demo is the luckiest output you ever got, so the most credible demo shows a *representative* case, or better, shows the product handling a genuinely hard input honestly. Demonstrated robustness beats demonstrated perfection.
- **Lead with the outcome in the first three seconds.** Autoplay-muted feeds mean the first frames must land the value without sound. Open on the result ("40% of tickets resolved before a human sees them"), then show the mechanism.
- **Keep it to the one core loop.** 30–60 seconds showing one complete valuable workflow beats a three-minute feature tour. The buyer needs to believe one thing works, not see everything.
- **Caption everything.** Muted autoplay is the default; on-screen text carries the narrative.
- **Human on judgment for the AI-generated parts.** The 2026 creative stack ([[03-wed-the-2026-ai-creative-stack|Block 4 Week 10 Wednesday]]) can produce the video fast, but an unreviewed AI-generated demo that looks like slop tells the buyer your *product* is unreviewed slop. Generate for speed; select and polish for credibility.

## Layer 5: Launching without a following

Most operators reading this do not have Justin Welsh's 360k LinkedIn followers, and the honest question is: how do you launch with 200 followers and no audience? The answer is that you borrow audiences and you concentrate, rather than broadcasting to a void.

**Borrow, don't broadcast.** With a small audience, your own posts reach few people, so the launch's reach must come from *other people's* audiences. Three legitimate mechanisms: (1) **relevant communities** where your buyer already gathers (Friday's whole lesson) — one thoughtful post to the right 2,000-person Slack reaches more real buyers than your 200 followers; (2) **direct outreach** (Thursday), a compliant sequence to hand-picked prospects who never needed to follow you; (3) **guesting on others' surfaces** — being featured in a relevant newsletter, quoted by an operator with reach, or hunted on PH by someone whose audience is your buyer. [[02-tue-content-strategy-and-platform-choice|Block 1 Week 2]]'s point stands: guesting and community are how small operators borrow the distribution they have not yet built.

**Concentrate, don't spread.** With scarce reach, spreading thin across five platforms guarantees invisibility on all of them. Pick the one surface where your buyer is densest and put the whole launch sequence there, plus one community, plus outbound. A 200-follower operator who runs a tight LinkedIn sequence to a buyer-dense audience, seeds one exactly-right community, and sends 20 compliant cold emails will out-launch a 2,000-follower operator who sprays a generic announcement across six platforms.

**The anti-slop edge is your equalizer.** This is the good news for the small operator. In a feed that is 41% machine-written, a genuinely human founder voice with real specifics is scarce and the platform now amplifies it.[^3][^7] You cannot out-reach a big account, but you can out-*substance* one, and in 2026 substance is what the rankers reward. Pieter Levels built PhotoAI to six figures a month "with no Product Hunt launch, no Hacker News submission, no press release — just Twitter" and a genuinely distinctive voice, not a large borrowed one at the start.[^8] The distinctive voice, not the follower count, was the asset, though note (per [[03-wed-building-in-public|Block 1 Week 2]]'s faucet thesis) that this is a product-shipper's path, and a services operator's content economy differs.

## Worked example: the triage-agent launch sequence

Continuing the support-triage agent (buyer: VP of Support; 900 LinkedIn followers, 200-person list). LinkedIn is the Primary social surface because the buyer lives there; X is Secondary (some support-tooling operators, useful for reach into the ecosystem).

- **Pre-launch (4 weeks):** two build-in-public LinkedIn posts a week showing the real work — "here's how the agent mishandled a 'reschedule two weeks out' request and how we fixed it" (a real limitation named, echoing the receipts discipline). Each ends with "shipping [date] — comment and I'll get you early access." This builds the warm list past the 200 already on it.
- **Launch day, LinkedIn:** the five-post sequence. Post 1 (founder story: the support-queue pain the operator lived), link in first comment. Post 2 (native 45-sec demo showing a typical triage run handling a messy ticket). Post 3 (an early user's "we cut first-response time from 14h to 1.1h" receipt). Post 4 (question: "What's the most broken part of your support triage today?"). Post 5 (evening thank-you with an honest note on the day).
- **Launch day, X:** one strong thread (the founder story, condensed, teaching the triage insight), plus real-time replies to everyone who engages; link in a reply, Premium held for the day.
- **Coordination:** Post 2 (demo) fires at midday, timed to the PH momentum window running in parallel (Tuesday), so the surfaces reinforce. The community seed (Friday) drops mid-morning.
- **Between posts:** reply within the hour, everywhere, personally. Every warm human goes into the list with role and context for Thursday's sequence and Week 11's interviews.

The whole sequence is executable by a 900-follower operator precisely because it is built on substance, conversation, and borrowing (community + outbound), not on reach the operator does not have.

## Runnable experiment: script the five-post sequence and stress-test the demo

**Task.** Produce two artifacts.

1. **The five-post launch sequence** for your product and your Primary social surface, written to the Layer-3 spec: Post 1 (founder story, problem-first, link-in-comment), Post 2 (demo, native-video caption teaching one insight), Post 3 (a specific receipt you actually have), Post 4 (a genuine conversation question), Post 5 (thank-you with an honest note). Each post must contain at least one *real specific* (a number, a named limitation, a real case) so it clears the slop classifier.
2. **The demo video plan**: the one core loop you will show, the first-three-seconds outcome hook, the typical-or-hard run you will use (not the best run), and the caption script.

Then run the anti-slop test in Claude: paste your five posts and prompt, *"You are LinkedIn's AI-slop classifier and a jaded VP of Support scrolling the feed. As the classifier, which of these five posts reads as generic/templated AI content I would demote, and exactly which phrase triggers it? As the VP, which post makes me stop scrolling and why, and which reads as a 🚀-announcement template I'd ignore?"*

**Pass bar.** (1) Every post survives the classifier test — if any post's demotion-trigger phrase is a launch cliché ("thrilled to announce," "game-changer," "🚀"), rewrite it around a real specific. (2) Post 1 leads with the problem, not the product, and could not have been written by someone who did not build it. (3) Your demo plan shows a typical or hard run and names why a best-run demo would read as a lie to your buyer. (4) None of the five posts has the link in the body. Time: 60–90 minutes.

## Common mistakes experts see

1. **The 🚀 announcement post.** "Thrilled to announce…" is the exact template LinkedIn's classifier demotes and audiences discount; lead with a problem-first story carrying real specifics instead.[^3]
2. **Link in the post body.** A 30–50%+ reach penalty on both platforms; substance in the post, link in the first comment or a reply.[^4]
3. **Clustering all posts at once.** The diversity cap and attention window punish it; space the five posts across the day and reply between them.[^5]
4. **A cherry-picked "best run" demo.** A technical audience assumes it is your luckiest output; show a typical or hard run to earn the credibility.[^6]
5. **Off-platform video links instead of native video.** Both platforms demote them and reward native uploads; upload the video, don't link to it.[^6]
6. **Performing build-in-public instead of showing the work.** The manufactured-vulnerability genre is fatigued and discounted; show real numbers and real limitations, not the genre's clichés.[^1]
7. **Broadcasting to a void when you have no following.** Borrow audiences (community, outbound, guesting) and concentrate on one surface rather than spraying a generic announcement across six.[^8]

## Reflection questions

1. Read your Post 1 aloud. Could someone who did *not* build your product have written it? If yes, it lacks the real specifics that both the classifier and the buyer reward — what would you add?
2. Where does your launch's reach actually come from if your own audience is small: which specific community, which specific prospects, which specific person's borrowed audience? If you cannot name them, you are planning to broadcast to a void.
3. The build-in-public authenticity debate: is your pre-launch content showing the real work or performing the genre? Name one post that is substance and one that is theater, and cut the theater.
4. Your demo shows a best run because it looks better. What is the exact risk that best-run demo creates with a technically-literate buyer, and what would the typical-run version cost you in polish but buy you in credibility?
5. On X, the two-way reply chain outweighs your own posts. What does that imply about how you should spend launch-day hours — posting more, or replying more? Does your plan reflect it?
6. If the slop classifier demoted your entire sequence tomorrow, which single post carries enough genuine human specificity to survive on its own? Why isn't every post at that bar?

## My take (reviewer lens)

**Simon Willison** would push on the demo-video advice from a transparency angle: for an AI product, the most credible thing you can publish is not a polished demo at all but a *reproducible* one, the actual prompt, the actual input, the actual output, warts included, because a demo the viewer can rerun themselves is worth more than any produced video, and the produced video invites exactly the "that's cherry-picked" suspicion he'd have himself. The lesson leans toward native video for algorithmic reasons (both platforms reward it), and Willison's caveat is the right tension: optimize the *format* for the feed, but let the *content* be as reproducible and honest as you can make it, because the technically-literate slice of your audience is the slice that converts and refers. **Jeremy Howard** would flag the anti-hype line hard: the whole five-post sequence risks becoming a more sophisticated version of the hype it critiques, and a founder who internalizes "substance beats slop" can still produce five substance-shaped posts that are, underneath, marketing. His fix, the one that keeps you honest — is that the launch sequence should teach the reader something *true and useful even if they never buy*, and if you strip out the product and the posts still have value, you have substance; if they collapse into nothing, you have slop with better production values. **The cohort peer** would ask the practical question the lesson glosses: what if you genuinely have no receipts yet, no users, no metrics, nothing for Post 3? The honest answer is that Post 3 then becomes the demo-as-receipt (the product doing the thing *is* the evidence at N=0) and that a launch with zero external validation is a signal to consider whether you should be running a smaller, quieter launch to *get* the first receipts before the loud one, which loops back to Monday's continuous-launch model, where the loud day is one event in a motion, not the first contact with reality.
