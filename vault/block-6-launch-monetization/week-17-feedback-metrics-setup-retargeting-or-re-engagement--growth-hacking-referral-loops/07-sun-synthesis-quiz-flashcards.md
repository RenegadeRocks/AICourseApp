# Synthesis, quiz & flashcards: making growth compound, and the Block 6 arc

## The week in one arc

One theme ran through all six days: **growth compounds only on top of retention,
and retention compounds only on top of a product people are very disappointed to
lose.** Everything else is machinery bolted to that foundation. You learned to
turn signal into roadmap, to win back the users who drifted without becoming the
villain, to see your product as loops instead of funnels, to do the referral math
honestly, and to assemble it all into one system that tells you when to stop
growing and go fix the product.

If you keep one sentence from each day:

- **[[01-mon-the-feedback-engine|Mon]]:** Feedback is ore, not the roadmap;
  segment the Sean Ellis survey the way Superhuman did (22% aggregate hid a
  path to 58%), and never build for the loudest user.
- **[[02-tue-retargeting-and-re-engagement|Tue]]:** Reactivation is the cheapest
  growth you own, fired by behavioral triggers not calendar blasts; build on owned
  channels because third-party retargeting fractured after 2025, and stay the
  right side of the FTC's dark-pattern line.
- **[[03-wed-growth-loops-vs-funnels|Wed]]:** Funnels leak and loops compound, but
  every loop decays via saturation and habituation; pick one dominant loop and
  instrument its amplification, cycle time, and weakest handoff.
- **[[04-thu-referral-and-virality-engineering|Thu]]:** Referral distributes love,
  it cannot manufacture it; even Dropbox ran k ≈ 0.5, below viral, so project k
  under 1, reward with product not cash, and reward retained actions to kill
  fraud.
- **[[05-fri-the-growth-system-and-honest-measurement|Fri]]:** Growth without
  retention is a leaky bucket; kill vanity metrics that can only go up, and know
  your stopping condition, the signal that sends you back to the product.
- **[[06-sat-build-the-growth-system|Sat]]:** Assemble the four components into one
  system with a measured loop and a saturating projection you would show an
  investor.

## Block 6 capstone recap: launch → monetize → grow

This is the final content week of Block 6, so step back and see the whole
go-to-market motion you have built across three weeks. Block 6 took the hardened,
validated product from Blocks 4 and 5 and turned it into a business that grows.

**Week 15 — Launch.** You planned a Product Hunt and social launch, published live,
and ran cold outreach. The launch is the *spike*: it proves people will try the
product, and it is a one-time event that does not compound on its own.
([[block-6-launch-monetization/week-15-plan-product-hunt-social-strategy--publish-live-cold-outreach|Week 15]]
(pending))

**Week 16 — Monetize.** You explored monetization paths, used AI in sales calls,
and built pricing strategy, tiers, and upsell mechanics. Monetization turns usage
into revenue: it is the number that decides whether growth is a business or a
charity.
([[block-6-launch-monetization/week-16-explore-monetisation-paths-using-ai-in-sales-calls-pricing-strategy--pricing-revenue-planning-pricing-tiers-upse|Week 16]]
(pending))

**Week 17 — Grow.** This week: the machinery that makes the launch and the pricing
compound. The feedback engine keeps the product worth spreading, re-engagement
plugs the leaks, loops turn users into more users, and referral amplifies genuine
love.

The arc is a sequence *and* a stack. As a sequence: you launch to prove demand,
monetize to prove a business, then grow to make it compound. As a stack: growth
sits on monetization sits on a launched, retained product. Pull any layer out and
the ones above it collapse. Grow without monetizing and you scale a hobby.
Monetize without a launch and you have priced a product nobody knows exists.
Launch without the retention and feedback foundation from Block 5 and you fill a
leaky bucket faster. The whole point of ordering the blocks this way is that each
layer earns the right to the next.

The unifying discipline across all three weeks is the one this course has hammered
since Block 4: **measure honestly, and let the measurement change what you do.**
Launch instrumentation (Block 4), retention analytics (Block 5), pricing metrics
(Week 16), and growth-loop efficiency (Week 17) are the same muscle applied at
each stage. The founders who compound are the ones who look at the number even
when it is telling them to stop and go back to the product.

**4. (Short answer)** Reactivating a lapsed user is often cited as how much cheaper

**5. (Code completion)** Complete the k-factor formula:

**6. (MCQ)** As of 2026, what is the state of third-party cookies in Chrome?

a) 2.5 b) 1.2 c) 0.5 d) 0.05

**9. (Short answer)** Why must a growth loop projection model saturation? What

**10. (Short answer)** What is the "leaky bucket" principle, and what does it imply

**11. (MCQ)** In a loop with handoffs produces-output 0.60, invites 0.40,
signup 0.70, which single improvement lifts the whole loop most?
a) Output 0.60→0.70 b) Invites 0.40→0.60 c) Signup 0.70→0.80 d) They are equal

**12. (Short answer)** What is the strongest single fraud guard for a referral

**13. (Short answer)** Give the "what happens when I stop?" test and explain what

**15. (Short answer)** Your PMF survey shows 34% aggregate, but one segment is at

## Answer key

**1.** b) Segmenting to the high-expectation customer. Filtering to the
high-expectation segment raised 22% to 32% with no product change; a year of
focused work took it to 58%.[^1]

**2.** "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" Options: (a) Very
disappointed (b) Somewhat disappointed (c) Not disappointed.[^2]

**3.** b) It correlates with historical rather than future growth and compresses an
11-point scale into three buckets, giving it low predictive validity; the verbatim
comment is worth more than the score.[^3]

**4.** Roughly 5 to 10 times cheaper. Caveat: it is a marketing-blog range, not an
audited constant; verify it against your own CAC and measure incrementality with a
holdout, because resurrected users partly self-select and would sometimes return
anyway.[^4]

**5.** `k = (refer_rate × invites_per_referrer) × invite_conversion`, i.e.
effective-invites-per-user × invite-conversion. Here: (0.30 × 3) × 0.15 = 0.135.[^5]

**6.** b) Not blocked by default. Google abandoned cookie deprecation, dropped even
the standalone choice prompt, and retired most Privacy Sandbox APIs on Oct 17,
2025; Safari/Firefox/Brave still block them.[^6]

**7.** Saturation (the loop burns through its addressable market) and habituation
(users tune out the once-novel share). Both lower effective k over time.[^7]

**8.** c) ≈ 0.5. Dropbox lifted k from about 0.24 to 0.56, still below the viral
threshold of 1.0. Referral was a strong amplifier, not self-sustaining
virality.[^8]

**9.** Because effective k falls as the market fills (fewer of the people your
users invite are still un-signed-up). Assume a constant k and you project a hockey
stick that never arrives, the most common growth-planning error.[^7]

**10.** Growth without retention is a leaky bucket: every growth lever multiplies
whatever retention you already have, so a loop on a low-retention product just
spreads churn. Implication: fix retention first, then build growth loops.
Retention is the multiplier, growth the multiplicand.

**11.** b) Invites 0.40→0.60. The weakest handoff is where one improvement moves
the whole product-loop amplification most (the amplification is the product of the
handoff rates).

**12.** Reward a retained action (a completed purchase, a subscription past a
threshold), not a bare signup. It removes the fraud incentive (fake signups become
worthless) and simultaneously filters for genuine users, improving quality.[^9]

**13.** Ask what happens to your growth if you stop the activity. If growth stops
immediately, it is a funnel or a hack. If it keeps compounding for a while, it is a
loop. Most founders cannot answer this about their own growth, which is itself the
diagnosis.

**14.** b) The 8th Circuit vacated it in July 2025 on procedural grounds, but the
FTC continues enforcing under Section 5 of the FTC Act and ROSCA (e.g. the Uber
UberOne complaint) and restarted rulemaking in March 2026, so transparency,
consent, and easy cancellation still bind.[^10]

**15.** Focus on the 58% segment (your high-expectation customer), protect what
they love, and fix the blockers keeping that segment's fence-sitters from "very
disappointed," then re-measure. It is called the Superhuman play.[^1]

**Scoring:** 13–15 correct, you are fluent, walk into the live session ready to
teach it. 10–12, solid, review the ones you missed. Below 10, re-read the two
weakest days before the live session and run `/deepen-lesson` on the topic.

## Flashcards

Q: What is the Sean Ellis PMF benchmark?
A: If ≥40% of recently-active users would be "very disappointed" without the
product, you likely have product/market fit. An empirical rule of thumb, not a
law.

Q: The single most important discipline when running the Sean Ellis test?
A: Segment, don't average. Superhuman found a 58% core hiding inside a 22%
aggregate.

Q: Who do you survey for a PMF test?
A: Only recently-active users who have experienced core value, not everyone who
ever signed up. Dead signups drag the score to zero and teach nothing.

Q: Why is feedback "ore, not the roadmap"?
A: Raw feedback carries recency bias, volume bias, and users describing solutions
instead of problems. The engine smelts it into weighted, segmented problems before
it touches the roadmap.

Q: The four stages of a feedback engine?
A: Collect, structure (problems not requests), prioritize by evidence weight, and
close the loop.

Q: Why distrust NPS as a roadmap input?
A: It correlates with historical not future growth, crushes an 11-point scale into
three buckets, and has low predictive validity. The verbatim comment beats the
number.

Q: The four user lifecycle states?
A: New, active, dormant (at-risk), churned. Each needs a different action;
dormant is usually the largest recoverable pool.

Q: Trigger-based re-engagement vs calendar blast?
A: A trigger fires on a specific behavior or its absence, personalized. A blast
sends everyone dormant the same email. Triggers convert far better.

Q: The highest-converting win-back trigger?
A: "You asked, we fixed it" to the exact users who churned citing a problem you
have since shipped a fix for. It also closes the feedback loop.

Q: Roughly how much cheaper is reactivation than acquisition?
A: About 5–10x, per lifecycle-marketing figures. Directional, not audited; verify
against your own CAC.

Q: State of third-party retargeting cookies in 2026?
A: Google abandoned deprecation; Chrome doesn't block them by default and retired
most Privacy Sandbox APIs (Oct 2025), while Safari/Firefox/Brave still block. Build
on owned channels, not third-party retargeting.

Q: The ethics test for a re-engagement flow?
A: Cancellation as easy as signup, frequency caps, instant unsubscribe, honest
(not fabricated) urgency, consent per channel. If it would embarrass you in a
deposition, redesign it.

Q: What happened to the FTC click-to-cancel rule?
A: Vacated by the 8th Circuit in July 2025 on procedural grounds, but enforcement
continues under Section 5/ROSCA and rulemaking restarted March 2026. The
principles still bind.

Q: Growth loop vs funnel, in one line?
A: A funnel turns money into users and stops when you stop paying; a loop turns
users into more users and compounds because output reinvests into input.

Q: The four loop families?
A: Viral/referral, content, paid, and product loops. Run one dominant loop, not
all four.

Q: The AI-product-native loop?
A: Output as marketing: the product generates a shareable artifact carrying your
brand ("Made with"), so every use is a potential acquisition event. Only works if
the output is good enough to be proud of.

Q: The Law of Shitty Clickthroughs?
A: Every marketing channel and loop degrades over time via habituation and
saturation. Banner CTR fell from ~70% to a tiny fraction. Loops need continuous
work against decay.

Q: The three numbers that measure a loop?
A: Amplification (branching factor), cycle time, and loop efficiency (conversion at
each handoff). The weakest handoff is where one fix moves the whole loop.

Q: The k-factor formula?
A: k = (effective invites per user) × (invite conversion rate) = refer_rate ×
invites_per_referrer × invite_conversion.

Q: k-factor regimes?
A: k>1 true viral (rare, temporary); 0.7–1.0 near-viral; 0.3–0.7 moderate;
0.15–0.25 where most successful companies land.

Q: Why does cycle time matter as much as k?
A: A faster loop compounds more often. Halving cycle time roughly doubles growth
velocity, so a low-k fast loop can beat a high-k slow loop.

Q: What k did Dropbox's famous referral program actually run?
A: About 0.5 (0.24→0.56). Below viral. A strong amplifier on a loved product, not
self-sustaining virality.

Q: Best referral reward type, and why?
A: More of your own product (Dropbox storage). Near-zero marginal cost, deepens
engagement, selects for genuine users. Cash (PayPal) attracts fraud and
mercenaries.

Q: The strongest referral fraud guard?
A: Reward a retained action, not a signup. Removes the fraud incentive and filters
for real users at once.

Q: When to ask for a referral?
A: At the moment of delight, right after the user experiences core value or a wow
output, never on a settings page.

Q: The "leaky bucket" principle?
A: Growth without retention is a leaky bucket; every growth lever multiplies
existing retention. Fix retention before building loops.

Q: What is a vanity metric, and its tell?
A: A number that only goes up and survives even as the business dies (cumulative
signups). The tell: a healthy metric can go down. If it can't warn you, it's a
prop.

Q: Sustainable vs mercenary growth?
A: Sustainable growth builds compounding loops and keeps working after you stop
pushing; mercenary growth buys numbers and stops when the budget does. Test: what
happens when you stop?

Q: The growth stopping condition?
A: If PMF is well below 40% or the retention curve decays toward zero, stop
optimizing growth and go back to the product. Growth spend on a leaky bucket is
wasted.

Q: The Block 6 arc in three words?
A: Launch, monetize, grow. A sequence and a stack: growth sits on monetization
sits on a launched, retained product.

Q: The input-metric tree?
A: The decomposition of a lagging north star into the levers a person can move,
with the retention multiplier explicitly in the middle so leaks upstream can't be
ignored.

Q: Why model incrementality in growth measurement?
A: Without a holdout, you credit a campaign for users who would have converted
anyway (selection effect). Compare to a control before declaring any lever a win.

## My take (reviewer lens)

**Jeremy Howard** would push on the whole week's benchmark citations: k-factor
regimes, the 5–10x reactivation figure, the 40% PMF line are all rules of thumb
dressed as constants, and a course should teach the reader to treat them as
hypotheses to test on their own data, not numbers to memorize. Agreed, and it is
why every quiz answer that quotes a benchmark also carries the caveat. The
transferable skill is the method (segment the survey, model saturation, measure
incrementality), not the specific numbers, which will drift.

**Michael Seibel** would say the capstone risks making growth sound more
systematic than it is for an early-stage founder, most of whom should be doing far
less growth engineering and far more talking to users and fixing the product. Fair,
and the week's own stopping condition agrees with him: the honest output of the
Saturday build is often "go back to the product." The system is for the moment you
have earned it, not before.
