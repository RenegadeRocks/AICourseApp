# Productizing your service: from hours to outcomes

## Why this matters

Today you decide whether your service becomes a product or stays a job. A custom
service where you scope every engagement from scratch and touch every deliverable
has a hard ceiling: your calendar. A productized service, fixed scope, fixed
price, fixed timeline, standardized delivery, breaks that ceiling by removing you
from the per-sale negotiation and, eventually, the per-project delivery. This
lesson gives you the model, the ladder from service to product, and the honest
pricing math. The artifact you leave with is the spec for one productized offer a
stranger could buy without talking to you. You will refine it Saturday.

## What a productized service actually is

A productized service is a service sold like a product: **fixed scope, fixed
price, standardized workflow, and (usually) a published price**.[^1] The
definition is best understood by contrast with what most service providers do. A
traditional agency or freelancer creates a custom scope and custom price for every
client, which means every sale requires discovery, a proposal, a negotiation, and
a bespoke delivery plan.[^1][^2] A productized service standardizes all of that:
the scope is decided once, the price is published, the delivery follows a repeated
process, and the client buys without a custom-scoping call.

The three canonical models, in increasing operational intensity:[^1][^2]

- **Fixed-scope project.** One defined outcome, one price, one timeline. "A
  10-page SEO audit in 5 business days for $2,000." Clean, but you resell each time.
- **Retainer.** A recurring package of defined work per month. "Monthly AI-ops
  maintenance: up to X automations maintained, Y hours of changes, for
  $3,500/month." The most common starting point for productized agencies, because
  it creates recurring revenue without the operational chaos of an unlimited
  queue.[^2]
- **Unlimited subscription.** A flat monthly fee for unlimited requests delivered
  one (or two) at a time from a queue. "Unlimited AI-automation requests,
  one active at a time, for $5,000/month." Highest perceived value, hardest to
  operate, because you carry queue and capacity risk.

Reported pricing bands for 2026, across multiple productized-service guides:
retainers commonly run **$1,500–$10,000/month**, and unlimited-model subscriptions
commonly run **$3,000–$15,000/month**, with solo-operator design subscriptions in
a credible **$2,500–$7,500/month** range depending on scope and turnaround.[^1][^2][^3]
Treat these as market anchors, not targets; your price comes from value and
capacity, covered below.

The load-bearing benefit is operational, not marketing. Standardization kills
scope creep, eliminates custom scoping on every sale, removes the sales call as a
prerequisite to buying, and creates what the productized camp calls an "assembly
line" for delivery.[^1][^2] The reported effect on margin is real: repeatable
productized work is cited at **55–70% margins**, well above bespoke agency
work.[^1] That margin claim is where Thursday's honesty audit begins, but the
mechanism is sound: you are amortizing the design of scope, process, and pricing
across many identical sales instead of redoing it every time.

## From hours to outcomes: the pricing shift

The deepest shift in productizing is what you sell. Hourly billing sells your
time, which caps your income at hours times rate and, worse, *punishes you for
getting faster*. The moment AI lets you deliver in two hours what took eight, an
hourly model cuts your revenue by 75% for the same outcome. This is the central
reason productization matters more in 2026 than it did in 2021: **AI decouples
value delivered from hours spent, and hourly pricing is on the wrong side of that
decoupling.**[^4]

Productized services price the *outcome* or the *package*, not the hours. The
client buys "a working lead-gen automation" or "a month of maintained ops," and
what it costs you to deliver, whether eight hours or, with AI, two, is your
business, not theirs. When you get faster, your margin expands instead of your
revenue collapsing. This is not a trick played on the client; the client always
preferred to buy the outcome and never wanted to buy your hours. Hourly billing
was always a proxy the client tolerated.

There is a genuine debate here about *how* to escape hourly, and it splits the
experts. We will engage it directly in the controversy section, because "productize
it" and "value-price it" are two different escapes from the hourly trap, and they
are not compatible on the same offer.

## Removing the founder from delivery

Productizing the *sale* (fixed scope, published price) is half the work.
Productizing the *delivery* is the half that actually frees you, and it is the
harder half. As long as you personally deliver every engagement, you have
productized your marketing but not your business; you have a very well-packaged
job.

Removing yourself from delivery has three stages, and you climb them in order:

1. **Templatize.** Turn your delivery into a repeatable process: a documented
   sequence of steps, with templates, checklists, and standard assets for each. The
   first time you do a thing, it is custom work. The second time, it should be a
   template you fill in. This is the raw material of the SOP library that Thursday
   builds on. If your delivery lives only in your head, it cannot be removed from
   you, so the first act of removing yourself is *writing it down*.

2. **Systematize with AI.** Replace the parts of the templated process that AI can
   do reliably: drafting, first-pass analysis, formatting, QA checklists, the
   status summary you built Tuesday. The productized camp is explicit that
   "standardization plus AI is where margins expand most without raising
   prices."[^2] The AI does not remove you from delivery; it removes the
   *low-judgment* parts of delivery, leaving you (or eventually a subcontractor)
   doing only the high-judgment parts.

3. **Delegate the process, not the outcome.** Once the process is templated and
   AI-augmented, a subcontractor or junior can run it, because the judgment has
   been encoded in the templates and the AI, and what remains is execution against
   a documented standard. This is the stage that converts a productized job into a
   productized business. It is Thursday's subject in depth.

The test of whether you have actually removed yourself: **could you take a
two-week vacation without delivery stopping?** If the honest answer is no, you have
a productized offer but not a productized business, and you are still the
single point of failure your buyer is (rightly) worried about.

## The service-to-product ladder

Productizing a service sits on a ladder, and knowing which rung you are on (and
which you are climbing to) prevents the most common strategic error: trying to
jump straight to pure product before you have learned what to build.

```
Rung 1  Custom service        — bespoke scope, you deliver, you sell each one
Rung 2  Productized service   — fixed scope/price, standardized delivery, you or team
Rung 3  Productized + AI      — same, but AI does the low-judgment delivery work
Rung 4  Hybrid (service+SaaS) — a tool does part of delivery; humans do the rest
Rung 5  Pure product (SaaS)   — the software delivers the outcome; no per-client labor
```

Each rung trades margin and scalability for delivery risk and distance from the
customer. The custom-service rung has the lowest margin and no scalability but the
tightest customer feedback loop and the lowest build risk. The pure-product rung
has the highest margin and unbounded scalability but requires you to have
correctly guessed what to build, at scale, before the market told you. The
productized-service rungs in the middle are where most service operators should
spend years, because **the service is how you learn what the product should be.**
Every engagement teaches you the real scope, the real objections, the real
delivery bottlenecks. Productizing that knowledge, then eventually encoding it in
software, is a lower-risk path to a product than guessing.

Tyler Tringas, who coined "Micro-SaaS" and invested in the productized-service
platform ManyRequests, embodies this ladder logic: he backs both the productized-
service model and the small-software model as complementary paths, with no-code
tools increasingly letting operators climb from rung 2 toward rung 5 without a
full engineering build.[^5] The ladder is not a queue you must fully traverse;
some businesses stop at rung 3 forever and are excellent businesses. But knowing
where you are stops you from pricing a rung-2 service like a rung-5 product, or
from abandoning a profitable service to chase a product you have not validated.

## Capacity vs scalability

The concept that trips up every service operator who thinks they have "scaled": a
productized service is more *efficient* but it is not automatically *scalable*, and
the two are different.

- **Efficiency** is margin per unit of delivery. Productization and AI improve
  efficiency: you deliver the same outcome with less labor. This is real and
  achievable and it is most of what this week delivers.
- **Scalability** is whether revenue can grow without your labor growing. A
  productized service where *you* still deliver is efficient but not scalable:
  your income is still capped by your capacity, just at a higher ceiling.

The unlimited-subscription model exposes this most sharply. It looks like a
product (flat monthly price, buy online, no sales call) but it is capacity-bound:
every subscriber consumes delivery capacity, so growth requires either more
capacity (hire) or more AI leverage per unit. The flagship example is instructive
and honest: DesignJoy, Brett Williams's one-person unlimited-design subscription,
is the poster child of the productized model and reached roughly **$3.1M ARR in
2024**, all solo. But its reported revenue **declined to around $1.7M ARR
(~$145K MRR) through 2025**.[^6][^7] The productized model did not fail; it hit the
capacity ceiling that a one-person unlimited-queue business structurally faces,
plus a more competitive market. This is the single most important cautionary data
point in the week: the most celebrated productized-service-of-one is a demonstration
of both the model's power *and* its capacity ceiling. Efficiency without a plan for
capacity is a higher, but still hard, ceiling.

> My take: the "solo founder, millions in revenue, no employees" story is
> seductive and mostly survivorship-biased. DesignJoy is real and impressive and
> its revenue still fell once it hit the ceiling that its structure guarantees.
> Plan for the ceiling from day one: either cap your subscriber count and raise
> price (protect margin, stay solo) or build the delivery team (Thursday) to grow
> capacity. Pretending the ceiling is not there is how productized founders burn
> out delivering their own "scalable" business at 60 hours a week.

## Worked example: designing one productized offer

Today's artifact is the spec for one offer a stranger could buy. Take a concrete
case: an AI-automation consultant who has done a dozen custom "connect your tools
with AI workflows" projects and wants to productize the most repeatable one.

**1. Pick the repeatable outcome.** Not "AI consulting." The specific, repeated
result: "We connect your support inbox to an AI triage-and-draft workflow so your
team answers tickets 3x faster." One outcome, repeatedly delivered, already
proven in custom work.

**2. Fix the scope.** Exactly what is and is not included. Included: connect up to
3 tools, one triage workflow, one draft-reply workflow, 2 weeks of tuning.
Excluded: custom model training, more than 3 tools, ongoing maintenance (that is a
separate retainer). The exclusions are as important as the inclusions; they are
what kills scope creep.

**3. Fix the price and timeline.** "$6,000, delivered in 10 business days."
Published. No custom quote. Anchor against the market bands (fixed-scope projects
and low-end retainers), then adjust for the value: if this saves the client a
support hire, $6,000 is cheap and you have room to raise it.

**4. Design the delivery SOP (outline).** The repeatable process: intake form →
tool-access checklist → workflow build from template → AI-drafted test cases →
human QA against eval checklist → client walkthrough → 2-week tuning. Note which
steps AI does (test cases, first-draft workflow config) and which require your
judgment (the QA gate, the client walkthrough). This outline is the seed of
Thursday's SOP library.

**5. Write the buy-without-a-call page.** The scope, price, timeline, what is
included/excluded, and a "buy" or "book" button. If a stranger could read it and
purchase without talking to you, it is productized. If they would need a call to
understand what they are getting, it is still custom.

**Pass bar for today:** a one-page productized-offer spec with a fixed
scope (inclusions and exclusions), a published price and timeline, a delivery-SOP
outline noting the AI-vs-judgment split, and a buy-page description a stranger
could act on. If any part requires a custom conversation to complete, mark it and
note what would let you standardize it.

## The controversy: productized service vs custom agency vs product

This is the week's central strategic debate, and it has named, credible positions
that genuinely disagree.

**The productized-service camp** (ManyRequests, Assembly's guide, the DesignJoy
lineage) argues productization is the correct escape from the hourly trap: fixed
scope and published price remove the sales bottleneck, standardization plus AI
expands margin, and the model is reachable for a solo operator today.[^1][^2] The
strong form: most service businesses should productize.

**The value-pricing camp**, led by Jonathan Stark (author of *Hourly Billing Is
Nuts*), argues the opposite escape. Stark holds that the highest-leverage move is
**value-based pricing**, setting the price on the *value of the outcome to a
specific buyer*, which requires a conversation before you name a price. Critically,
he argues productized services and value pricing are **fundamentally different and
not combinable on the same offering**: a productized service requires a *published*
price, while value pricing requires a *conversation* to discover the buyer's
specific willingness to pay.[^8] The strong form: publishing a fixed price leaves
money on the table for high-value buyers, and you should value-price custom
engagements instead of productizing them down to a commodity price.

**The pure-product camp** argues both of the above are still labor-bound, and the
real prize is software that delivers the outcome with zero marginal labor (rung 5).
The strong form: a productized service is a stepping stone at best and a trap at
worst, because you can spend years optimizing a business that will always be capped
by capacity when you could have built a product.

Where the honest operator lands: these are not universal truths; they are matched
to *buyer type and outcome variance*. Productize when the outcome is standardizable
and the buyers are numerous and similar (Stark's published-price weakness does not
bite when the value is roughly the same across buyers). Value-price when outcomes
vary enormously by buyer and each engagement is high-stakes and bespoke (Stark is
right that a published price on a $500K-value outcome is malpractice). Build product
when you have proven, through service, exactly what to build and the market is large
enough to justify the build risk. The mistake is treating any one camp's strong
form as gospel. Your offer, your buyers, and your outcome variance decide which
escape from hourly is correct, and a mature service business often runs more than
one: a productized entry offer *and* value-priced custom engagements for large
accounts.

## Common mistakes experts see

1. **Productizing the sale but not the delivery.** Fixed price and published scope
   with you still hand-delivering every engagement is a well-packaged job, not a
   productized business. Templatize and delegate delivery, or you have not removed
   the bottleneck.

2. **Pricing on hours in an AI-accelerated world.** The moment AI makes you 4x
   faster, hourly pricing cuts your revenue 75% for the same value. Price the
   outcome or the package so speed expands margin instead of shrinking revenue.[^4]

3. **Under-specifying exclusions.** A productized scope that says what is included
   but not what is excluded invites scope creep back in. The exclusions are what
   make the fixed price hold.

4. **Confusing efficiency with scalability.** A productized service you still
   deliver is efficient, not scalable. Your income is still capped by capacity, at
   a higher ceiling. Plan for the ceiling (cap and raise price, or build a team).[^6]

5. **Jumping to pure product before the service taught you what to build.** The
   service is your lowest-risk market research. Productize and learn before you
   bet a year of engineering on an unvalidated product.

6. **Publishing a fixed price on a high-variance, high-value outcome.** Stark's
   critique bites here: if the value ranges from $5K to $500K across buyers, a
   published price is malpractice. Match the pricing model to the outcome
   variance.[^8]

## Reflection questions

1. Which single repeatable outcome from your custom work is the best productization
   candidate, and why that one? What are its natural exclusions?
2. Could you take a two-week vacation without your delivery stopping? If not, which
   stage of founder-removal (templatize / systematize / delegate) are you missing?
3. Which rung of the service-to-product ladder are you on, and which one are you
   actually trying to climb to? Is that ambition validated or aspirational?
4. Is your best offer better productized (published price, standard scope) or
   value-priced (conversation, bespoke price)? What does the *variance in outcome
   value across your buyers* tell you?
5. Where is your capacity ceiling, and what is your explicit plan for it: cap and
   raise price, or build the team?

## My take (reviewer lens)

**Michael Seibel** would cut through the ladder abstraction: "Which offer can you
sell to a stranger this week without a call? Build that page, put a price on it,
and see if anyone buys. The rest is theory." He is right that the productized
spec is only real once a stranger buys it, and the whole point of a published
price is that it is testable immediately. Do not spend the week perfecting the SOP
for an offer no one has purchased.

**Chip Huyen** would push on the AI-margin claim underneath the whole model. The
"standardization plus AI expands margins" line is doing a lot of work, and she
would want it measured, not asserted: what is your *actual* delivery cost per unit
with AI in the loop, including the human QA time that AI-generated work requires?
Thursday's lesson is the honest accounting she would demand, and the DesignJoy
revenue decline is the reminder that the margin story has a capacity ceiling the
enthusiasts skip.

**Jonathan Stark** (the extra domain voice, verified real: author of *Hourly
Billing Is Nuts*, value-pricing consultant) would object to the whole framing that
productizing is the default answer. His position deserves to be steelmanned, not
dismissed: for high-value, high-variance outcomes, a published price is money left
on the table, and the more sophisticated move is to value-price custom work.[^8]
The lesson's resolution, match the pricing model to outcome variance, is the
honest synthesis, but Stark would insist most operators productize prematurely and
commoditize outcomes that a conversation could have priced at multiples.
