# Agency vs SaaS: the two system topologies

## Why this matters

The same components you mapped yesterday can be arranged into two very different
machines, and the arrangement determines your cash flow, your ceiling, your
freedom, and how the next model release affects you. An agency and a SaaS
product are not two different businesses so much as two topologies of the same
subsystems. Today you decide which topology you are building, argue it against
the strongest counter-case, and learn the ladder that lets you move between them
without betting the company. Get this wrong and you spend a year building a
product nobody will buy, or scaling an agency that caps at your own exhaustion.

## Core content

### The two topologies, defined by what you sell

The dividing line is not "do you use software." Both use software. The line is
**what the customer is actually buying and what scales when you add a customer.**

- **Agency (services).** You sell outcomes delivered by people (you, plus AI and
  contractors). Each new customer consumes human capacity. Revenue is a function
  of capacity times price. You scale by adding capacity or raising price.
- **SaaS (product).** You sell access to software the customer operates
  themselves. Each new customer consumes near-zero marginal human capacity.
  Revenue is a function of customers times price, and the machine that produces
  the product runs once for everyone.

Everything else — cash flow, margins, valuation, how fragile you are to the next
model release — falls out of that one difference. Hold it in your head as you
read the rest.

### The same subsystems, arranged differently

Yesterday's three subsystems (demand, delivery, money) exist in both topologies.
They are shaped differently.

| Subsystem | Agency | SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | High-touch: outreach, referrals, founder brand, proposals | Self-serve: content, SEO, product-led signups, ads |
| Delivery | Per-client work, bounded by capacity | Build once, ship to all; the "delivery" is deployment and uptime |
| Money | Project fees + retainers; cash up front, lumpy | Subscriptions; smooth, recurring, compounding via retention |

Notice that the demand subsystem in an agency leans hard on the founder brand
and the thought-leadership engine you build this week, while in SaaS the same
engine feeds a self-serve funnel. This is why the content engine is not a
distraction from either topology — it is the demand subsystem's fuel in both.

### Cash flow and scaling: the honest differences

This is where the topologies diverge most, and where founders romanticize the
wrong one.

**Agency cash flow is immediate and lumpy.** You sign a client, invoice a
deposit, and cash arrives this month. That is an enormous, underrated advantage:
an agency can be profitable in month one and fund everything else from revenue.
The cost is a hard ceiling — your revenue is capacity times price, and capacity
is expensive to add and does not compound. AI raises the ceiling by making each
person more productive, but it does not remove it. The deeper 2026 risk is
margin compression: as "services-as-software" tools let clients self-serve work
you used to bill for, agency pricing on commoditized tasks trends toward the cost
of the tool.

**SaaS cash flow is delayed and then compounding.** You build for months with no
revenue, launch, and grow slowly (the "law of shitty clickthroughs" — every
acquisition channel decays as it saturates, so early traction understates the
grind ahead).[^1] But once it works, revenue recurs without re-selling, and it
compounds through net revenue retention: existing customers expanding is growth
you did not have to acquire. In 2026, vertical SaaS focused on a single industry
frequently posts net revenue retention above 130%, meaning the customer base
grows revenue even with zero new logos.[^2]

**The survival and profitability data (2026).** For bootstrapped founders
specifically, the picture favors disciplined product economics: 2026 analyses
put bootstrapped startups at roughly 3× the profitability odds of VC-backed peers
in the first three years, spending about a quarter as much on customer
acquisition, at similar growth rates (~20% vs ~22% median), with five-year
survival near 35–40% versus 10–15% for VC-backed.[^3] Read that carefully: it is
an argument for bootstrapped discipline and low CAC, not automatically for SaaS
over agency. An agency *is* the ultimate low-CAC, high-survival bootstrap. The
data cuts toward "own your economics," which both topologies can do.

### The hybrid ladder (the answer most readers actually want)

The agency-vs-SaaS framing is a false binary for most people at your stage. The
real path is a ladder, and you can stand on more than one rung at once.

1. **Pure agency / consulting.** Trade time for money. Highest cash-flow
   certainty, lowest scale, fastest to start. Where most readers are or should
   start.
2. **Productized service.** A fixed scope, fixed price, repeatable-delivery
   offer — the rung you built in [[block-7-onboarding-tracking/week-19-build-async-client-dashboard-or-project-tracking-for-agency--productizing-your-service-community-market-research/03-wed-productizing-your-service|B7W19]]. Same "people deliver it" model, but standardized enough that margins climb and delivery starts to feel like a product.
3. **Software-enabled service.** You build internal tooling to deliver the
   productized service faster; clients still buy the outcome, but your margins
   look increasingly like software. The tooling is the seed of a product.
4. **SaaS with a services layer.** The tooling becomes the product; services
   become onboarding and premium support. This is where most successful
   bootstrapped B2B software actually lives, not at the pure-SaaS extreme.
5. **Pure self-serve SaaS.** The customer never talks to a human. Highest scale,
   hardest to reach, and rarely the right target for a solo founder's first
   product in a B2B niche.

Rob Walling calls a version of this the "stair-step" approach: start with
something small and cash-generating, use the cash and the audience to fund the
next, bigger, more product-like step, rather than leaping straight to a
venture-scale SaaS with no runway.[^4] The canonical proof is 37signals: Jason
Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson built Basecamp out of the internal tooling of
a web-design agency, then let the product eat the agency once it was clearly the
better business. The agency funded and de-risked the product.

### Choosing based on your constraints, not your fantasy

The internet romanticizes SaaS (passive income, high multiples) and looks down
on agencies (trading time for money). Ignore the status ranking and choose on
constraints.

**Choose agency-first if:** you need cash now, you do not yet know the exact
product to build, your edge is a relationship or a reputation in a niche, or you
want to learn the domain deeply before betting a year of building on it. The
agency is a paid education in your customers' problems, and that education is the
single best input to a product later.

**Choose SaaS-first if:** you have real runway (savings or an existing income),
you have already validated a specific, repeated, painful problem — ideally *from*
doing the service work — and the problem is stable enough that a model release
will not flatten it. The 2026 filter is unforgiving here: micro-SaaS built on a
thin wrapper over a frontier model is a "countdown timer," not a business; the
durable ones solve a workflow or own data the base model does not have.[^5]

**Choose the ladder (recommended for almost everyone):** agency or productized
service now for cash and learning, with an explicit plan to build tooling that
becomes a product once you have seen the same problem across ten clients. This
is the path with the best risk-adjusted odds for a bootstrapped AI builder in
2026, and it is what your complete-system map is secretly preparing you for.

### The controversy: agency-first vs product-first for AI builders in 2026

This is a live, real disagreement with money on both sides. Hold both positions.

**The product-first camp** argues that agencies are a trap: you build a business
that cannot scale past your capacity, your revenue vanishes the moment you stop
selling, and in 2026 AI is specifically compressing agency margins as clients
self-serve. Better, they say, to endure the cash-flow desert and build an asset
that compounds and could one day sell for a multiple. The 2026 data on
bootstrapped SaaS profitability, low CAC, and 130%+ vertical NRR is their
Exhibit A.[^2][^3]

**The agency-first camp** argues that this romanticizes SaaS and ignores base
rates. Most SaaS products never reach ramen profitability; most founders run out
of runway before they find product-market fit. An agency is profitable in month
one, teaches you the customer's problem better than any survey, and funds the
product build from revenue instead of savings. In an era of monthly model
releases, the argument sharpens: services adapt to each new capability
instantly, while a product built on last quarter's model can be obsoleted
overnight. The bootstrapping-in-2026 practitioners building without VC lean this
way — start with cash-generating work, let the product emerge from it.[^6]

**The resolution is the ladder, plus a timing rule.** Do not choose the extreme;
choose the rung that matches your runway and your certainty, and move up only
when a specific signal fires. The signal to climb from service toward product is
concrete: you have seen the *same* painful problem across roughly ten clients,
you have built internal tooling to solve it faster, and clients start asking if
they can just buy the tool. Until that signal fires, product-building is a
guess funded by your savings. After it fires, staying a pure agency is leaving a
compounding asset on the table.

> My take: for a reader of this course with fewer than a handful of clients, the
> agency-vs-SaaS debate is mostly a procrastination engine. The correct move is
> almost always "do paid service work in a tight niche, instrument what you learn,
> and let the product reveal itself." The people who agonize over the topology
> in the abstract are usually avoiding the concrete work of selling this week.

## Worked example: the same components, two machines

Take the RAG knowledge-assistant from yesterday's Priya example and build it as
each topology.

**As an agency.** Priya sells "we build and maintain your firm's AI knowledge
assistant" for a $12k setup and $2k/month retainer. Demand: her LinkedIn and
referrals. Delivery: she and her partner build each one, reusing the delivery
engineering from [[block-4-test-validate-package/week-09-packaging-selling-your-ai-agents--create-your-first-sellable-agent-package/03-wed-delivery-engineering-one-build-many-customers|B4W09]]. Cash: positive from month one, capped at maybe ten clients before they are underwater in maintenance. Model-release risk: low; a better model just makes her deliverables better.

**As a SaaS.** The same assistant becomes a self-serve product law firms sign up
for at $499/month, configure themselves, and run without Priya. Demand: content,
SEO, a free tier. Delivery: build once, then it is uptime and support. Cash:
negative for six-plus months of building and slow early signups (the shitty-
clickthrough grind[^1]), then compounding via NRR as firms add seats and
matters. Model-release risk: higher if the product is a thin wrapper; lower if
it owns the firm's document workflow and data.

**As the ladder (what Priya should actually do).** Keep the agency running for
cash. Build the configuration tooling she already uses internally into a
self-serve product. Offer it first to the eleven clients she has — who trust her
brand and have the embedded workflow — as a lower-touch tier. The agency funds
the build; the client base de-risks the launch; the brand does the demand. That
is the stair-step, and it is available to her precisely *because* she started as
an agency.

## Common mistakes experts see

1. **Choosing SaaS for the status, not the constraints.** "Passive income" is
   the marketing; six months of no revenue and a slow grind is the reality.
   Choose on runway and validated pain, not on the multiple you fantasize about.
2. **Choosing agency and pretending the ceiling isn't there.** Capacity times
   price is a real cap. If you never build tooling or productize, you have bought
   yourself a demanding job with no exit.
3. **Leaping straight to pure self-serve SaaS.** Most bootstrapped B2B software
   lives at "SaaS with a services layer," not at the no-human extreme. Reaching
   for pure self-serve first usually means dying in the demand desert.
4. **Building a thin wrapper as a "SaaS."** In 2026 a wrapper over a frontier
   model with no data, workflow, or community moat is a countdown timer.[^5]
   Ask the model-release question before you build.
5. **Never climbing the ladder.** Staying a pure agency after you have clearly
   seen the same problem ten times and built the tooling is leaving a compounding
   asset unbuilt out of comfort.
6. **Ignoring cash flow in the topology choice.** The topology *is* a cash-flow
   decision first. Agencies are cash-positive early and capped; SaaS is
   cash-negative early and compounding. Pick the one your bank balance can
   survive.

## Reflection questions

1. Which topology are you actually building right now, as opposed to the one you
   tell people you are building? What does your calendar say versus your pitch?
2. What is your runway in months? How does that number constrain which rung of
   the ladder you can stand on today?
3. If you are doing service work, what is the *same* painful problem you have now
   seen across multiple clients? Is it stable enough to survive a year of
   product-building and the next few model releases?
4. Where on the five-rung ladder are you, and what specific signal would tell you
   it is time to climb the next rung?
5. If a frontier model shipped tomorrow that did 30% of your current
   deliverable for free, would that help you (agency) or gut you (thin wrapper)?
   What does the answer tell you about your topology choice?

## My take (reviewer lens)

**Michael Seibel** would cut through the whole framework: at your stage the
answer is almost always "start as a service, because you can start it Monday and
get paid Friday, and you will learn the customer's problem better than any
amount of product strategy." He would be impatient with the ladder's neatness —
in practice you climb it by getting punched in the face by reality, not by
waiting for a clean signal. He is right that the signal is fuzzier than the
lesson implies; the discipline is to keep selling while you watch for it.

**swyx**, whose lens is the AI-engineering zeitgeist, would push on the
"services-as-software" compression as the real 2026 story: the agencies that die
are the ones billing for work a tool now does for $20/month; the ones that thrive
move up-market to judgment, integration, and outcomes the tool cannot deliver
alone. His correction is that the topology question is downstream of a sharper
one — *which layer of the value chain are you defending as AI eats the layer
below you?*

**A cohort peer** who tried SaaS-first and ran out of runway would tell you the
unglamorous truth the data hides: the median SaaS attempt is not a compounding
asset, it is an abandoned repo. The survival stats are real but they are
averages over survivors and quitters; your personal outcome is binary. Starting
with cash-generating work is not a lack of ambition, it is how you buy enough
shots on goal to get lucky.
