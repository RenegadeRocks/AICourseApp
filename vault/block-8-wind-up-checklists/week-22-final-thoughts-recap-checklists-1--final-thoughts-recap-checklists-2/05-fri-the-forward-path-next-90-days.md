# The forward path: your next 90 days

## Why this matters (operator framing)

A course ends the moment it stops changing what you do. The single most common way
to waste everything you just learned is to keep learning — another cohort, another
tutorial, another model release to read about — instead of shipping. This lesson
gives you a concrete 90-day plan template keyed to your actual stage, the one
highest-leverage action for that stage, an honest read on your odds, and the
accountability structure that keeps a plan from dying in a notebook. No hype. Some
of these paths do not work out, and you deserve the real numbers.

## Prerequisites

[[02-tue-the-master-build-checklist|Tuesday's audit]] — your score and gap list are
the raw material for today's plan — and [[04-thu-staying-current-re-verification|Thursday's re-verification practice]], because a 90-day plan built on stale facts is a 90-day detour.

## First, name your stage

Your plan depends entirely on where you actually are, not where you wish you were.
Use your Tuesday audit band to place yourself.

- **Idea** (audit 0–25, mostly Stage 1–2 work): you have a hypothesis and maybe a
  prototype, no paying customers.
- **Building** (audit 26–45): you have a product and a few early customers or
  pilots, but no repeatable revenue.
- **Launched** (audit 46–60): you have a real business with recurring revenue and
  a working motion, run largely by you.
- **Scaling** (audit 61–70): systematized, and the question is growth without
  breaking.

Everything below branches on this. Read your stage carefully and skim the others.

## The one highest-leverage action per stage

If you do nothing else in 90 days, do this.

**Idea → get one person to pay.** Not a waitlist, not a like. One payment, or one
signed pilot with money attached. Paul Graham's "do things that don't scale" is
the whole instruction here: recruit users by hand, do the unscalable work to make
the first ones succeed, and treat the first sale as the only validation that
counts.[^1] The failure mode at this stage is building more; the fix is selling
what you have. → [[block-1-problem-solving-outreach/week-01-getting-your-first-client--how-to-plan-scope-and-sell-ai-projects/06-sat-selling-ai-objections-and-closing|selling and closing]]

**Building → make the revenue repeatable.** You have proof someone pays; now make
the motion reproducible. One channel that reliably produces leads, one pricing
model that clears margin, one delivery process you do not reinvent per customer.
The failure mode is a scattered pile of one-off deals; the fix is a repeatable
motion. → [[block-4-test-validate-package/week-09-packaging-selling-your-ai-agents--create-your-first-sellable-agent-package/03-wed-delivery-engineering-one-build-many-customers|delivery engineering]]

**Launched → systematize yourself out of the bottleneck.** You are the constraint.
The highest-leverage move is turning the work in your head into SOPs and handing
the reproducible parts to a person or an agent. The failure mode is a job that
looks like a business; the fix is an operating system that runs without you. →
[[block-7-onboarding-tracking/week-20-sops-for-onbaording-delivery-growth--build-community-paid-inner-circle/01-mon-sops-the-operating-system-of-a-scaling-business|SOPs as an operating system]]

**Scaling → pick one loop and pour into it.** You have the machine; now find the
one growth loop with the best unit economics and concentrate. The failure mode is
diffuse effort across five channels; the fix is one compounding loop instrumented
honestly. → [[block-6-launch-monetization/week-17-feedback-metrics-setup-retargeting-or-re-engagement--growth-hacking-referral-loops/03-wed-growth-loops-vs-funnels|growth loops]]

## The 90-day plan template

A 90-day horizon is long enough to ship something real and short enough that you
cannot hide. Structure it as three 30-day sprints, each with one primary outcome.

**Sprint 1 (days 1–30) — close your worst gap.** Take the single 0-or-1 item from
Tuesday that most blocks revenue and make it a 2. One outcome, not five. Write it
as a falsifiable result: "10 paying pilots," not "grow the business."

**Sprint 2 (days 31–60) — execute your stage's highest-leverage action.** From the
section above. This is the core of the quarter.

**Sprint 3 (days 61–90) — compound or correct.** If sprints 1–2 worked, build the
loop or SOP that makes the gain repeatable. If they did not, run the honest
post-mortem: was the bet wrong, the execution wrong, or the market wrong? Thursday's
kill-condition discipline applies to your own plan.

For each sprint, write down: the one outcome, the weekly leading indicator you will
watch, and the one thing you will *not* do (your explicit no-list). The no-list is
what protects the plan from the traps below.

## The traps that eat the plan

**Tutorial hell / the perpetual learner.** The most dangerous trap for someone who
just finished a 26-week course. Learning feels like progress and carries no risk
of rejection, so it becomes a hiding place. Signs: you have completed this course
and are shopping for the next one before shipping anything from this one; you can
name six models but have zero paying customers; your "research" always concludes
you need to learn one more thing first. The cure is a shipping cadence with a
public deadline. You have enough. The field will change under you regardless
(Thursday), so waiting to "finish learning" is waiting for a finish line that does
not exist.

**Rebuilding instead of selling.** The urge to refactor, redesign, or re-platform
when the actual blocker is that you have not asked enough people to pay. Every
hour on the codebase that should have been an hour on the phone.

**Diffusion.** Running four channels, three pricing experiments, and two product
directions at once. Solo operators lose to diffusion far more than to competition.
One loop, one motion, one primary outcome per sprint.

**Chasing the model release.** A new model dropped; you spend a week migrating for
a marginal gain no customer asked for. Model choice is a weeks-scale fact you
re-verify on a cadence (Thursday), not a reason to stop shipping.

## When to hire, partner, or raise

The honest defaults for a solo AI operator, stated plainly.

**Hire when the constraint is durable and defined.** Not when you are busy — busy
is temporary and often a signal to systematize (SOPs) or automate (agents) before
adding headcount. Hire when there is a repeatable, documented role an SOP already
describes and the revenue covers it. In an AI-native operation, your first "hire"
is often an agent for the reproducible work, and a human for the judgment. →
[[block-7-onboarding-tracking/week-19-build-async-client-dashboard-or-project-tracking-for-agency--productizing-your-service-community-market-research/04-thu-delivery-systems-and-the-ai-augmented-team|the AI-augmented team]]

**Partner when the gap is a capability, not a task.** A partner is a permanent,
equity-level commitment; use it to acquire something you structurally lack
(distribution, a domain, a complementary skill), not to offload work you could
document. Partnerships formed to avoid loneliness or work usually end badly.

**Raise only if the business is capital-constrained rather than validation-
constrained.** Most solo AI businesses in 2026 are not capital-constrained — the
tools are cheap and margins are high once economics work. Raising to fund a search
for product-market fit converts a low-risk bootstrap into a high-pressure bet. The
honest default for most graduates of this course is: do not raise; the business
either works on customer revenue or it is not ready.

## The honest probabilities

No hype. Here is the realistic conversation.

Most new businesses do not reach durable revenue. Standard startup base rates put
multi-year survival well below half, and solo ventures are not exempt.[^2] The AI
layer changes the shape but not the physics: cheaper to start, so more attempts,
so more failures, and the same distribution rules apply. Y Combinator and operator
voices frame AI-native services as an unusually large opportunity, with services
running roughly 70–80% margin and productized software pushing toward 90%+.[^3] The
opportunity is real. The base rate is still humbling, and anyone selling you a
near-certain outcome is selling you something.

What the base rate does *not* mean: that your odds are fixed. The whole method of
this course — validate before building, price on value, retention before growth,
eval-gate, systematize — exists to move you from the failing majority toward the
surviving minority. Ethan Mollick's adoption research points the same way: the
separating factor is consistent working practice, not raw talent or the newest
model.[^5] Recall the MIT NANDA finding that roughly 95% of enterprise
generative-AI pilots showed no measurable impact.[^4] Your entire training was
about being in the 5%. The realistic frame: your probability is low in the base
rate and materially higher if you execute the discipline, and the single biggest
lever on it is whether you ship at all. Most people who fail this stage fail by not
shipping, not by shipping and losing.

**The controversy, stated honestly:** some operators argue the AI wave makes solo
success meaningfully more likely — cheaper builds, agent leverage, a $10k-MRR
micro-SaaS as a life-changing solo outcome.[^3] Others argue the same cheapness
floods every niche and compresses margins to nothing. Both are partly right. The
resolution is not a number; it is that averages are irrelevant to you. You are
one operator running one specific bet with a method most people do not have. Play
your bet well and re-verify the landscape quarterly; ignore the aggregate.

## Accountability: what makes a plan survive contact with reality

A plan in a private notebook has a short life. The structures that keep it alive:

- **A public commitment.** Building in public, a cohort, or a peer who will ask.
  The course's own community and inner-circle structures exist for exactly this
  compounding accountability. → [[block-7-onboarding-tracking/week-20-sops-for-onbaording-delivery-growth--build-community-paid-inner-circle/04-thu-building-community-the-compounding-moat|community as a moat]]
- **A weekly leading indicator you actually check.** Outcomes are lagging; watch
  the leading number (calls booked, pilots started) weekly.
- **A single accountability partner** from your cohort who sees your sprint
  outcomes and calls you on the traps above. → [[block-1-problem-solving-outreach/week-02-importance-of-personal-branding--niche-discovery/03-wed-building-in-public|building in public]]
- **A dated review.** End of each 30-day sprint: hit or miss, and why. Not a
  feeling — a written verdict.

## Worked example

A "Building"-stage operator (audit 38) writes her quarter. Sprint 1: her worst gap
is pricing on vibes (Tuesday item 21 = 0), so the outcome is "a value-metric price
recomputed against current token costs, live on the site." Leading indicator:
proposals sent at the new price. No-list: no new features. Sprint 2 (highest-
leverage: make revenue repeatable): "one outbound channel producing 5 qualified
calls/week." Sprint 3: "an onboarding SOP so I stop reinventing delivery." Her
accountability: a weekly post in her cohort channel and one peer who reviews each
sprint verdict. Ninety days, three outcomes, one no-list per sprint.

**Pass bar:** you have a dated three-sprint plan with one falsifiable outcome per
sprint, a weekly leading indicator, a no-list, and one named accountability
structure. Saturday's tool generates the scaffold; today you supply the judgment.

## Common mistakes experts see

- **Planning five outcomes per sprint.** One. Solo capacity is the binding
  constraint.
- **Making learning the plan.** "Study X" is not a 90-day outcome. Shipping is.
- **Vague outcomes.** "Grow" is not falsifiable. "10 paying pilots" is.
- **Raising or hiring to escape validation.** Both convert a survivable bootstrap
  into a pressured bet before the business has earned it.
- **No accountability.** A private plan and a public plan have very different
  survival rates.
- **Ignoring the base rate, or being crushed by it.** Both are errors. Respect it,
  then play your specific bet well anyway.

## Reflection questions

1. What is your stage, honestly, by Tuesday's band?
2. What is the single outcome of your Sprint 1, stated falsifiably?
3. Which trap are you most personally prone to — tutorial hell, rebuilding,
   diffusion, or chasing releases?
4. Are you validation-constrained or capital-constrained? What does that say about
   raising?
5. Who is your one accountability partner, and have you actually asked them yet?

## My take (reviewer lens)

**Seibel** would strip this down further. His instinct: stages, sprints, and
no-lists risk becoming planning theater, and the only thing that matters at Idea
stage is "did someone pay you this week." He is right that the plan is worthless if
it delays the first sale; the template exists to force shipping, not to replace it.
If in doubt, do the ugly thing that gets a payment.

**Mollick** would want the probabilities framed as a distribution with agency, not
a single grim number. His research supports the "base rate low, execution moves
it" framing, and he would push harder on the finding that consistent practice, not
talent, separates outcomes. Lean into his optimism-with-evidence, not doom.

**A cohort peer** who has actually shipped would call out the "when to raise"
section as slightly abstract for someone at Idea stage who will not raise for
years. Fair — most readers should treat hire/partner/raise as future reference and
spend their attention on the highest-leverage action for their real stage.
